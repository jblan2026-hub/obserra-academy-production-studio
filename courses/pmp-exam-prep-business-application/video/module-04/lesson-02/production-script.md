## Scene 3. What the integrated plan does, 03:35 to 06:55

**VISUAL**

An integrated plan appears with four layers.

### Strategic layer

Purpose, outcomes, benefits, value, success criteria, sponsor intent, and alignment to strategy.

### Delivery layer

Scope or backlog, milestones, releases, schedule, resources, procurement, quality, risk, communications, and technical integration.

### Governance layer

Decision rights, approval thresholds, change control, risk acceptance, reporting, compliance, assurance, and escalation.

### Transition and learning layer

Acceptance, operational readiness, knowledge transfer, benefit ownership, support, records, lessons, and closure.

**NARRATOR**

The integrated plan explains how the project will be executed, monitored, controlled, adapted, governed, and closed.

It may be one controlled document, a connected set of artifacts, or a digital planning environment. The format matters less than coherence, accessibility, ownership, traceability, and decision usefulness.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Integration means that a material change in one part of the project is visible in every affected part.

## Scene 4. The integration map, 06:55 to 11:20

**VISUAL**

A network map connects core plan elements.

### Scope and value

What work and outcomes are included, excluded, prioritized, and accepted.

### Schedule and releases

When work, decisions, dependencies, integration, and value are expected.

### Cost and funding

What resources are required, when funds are needed, and how uncertainty and reserves are governed.

### Resources

Who and what is needed, with availability, skills, capacity, facilities, and constraints.

### Quality and assurance

Which standards, criteria, reviews, tests, and evidence demonstrate fitness and compliance.

### Risk and issues

Which uncertainties and realized problems can affect objectives and what responses are planned.

### Procurement and suppliers

Which external capabilities, contracts, incentives, lead times, acceptance terms, and dependencies matter.

### Stakeholder and communication

Who must understand, participate, decide, adopt, or receive evidence.

### Compliance and security

Which obligations, controls, records, and approvals constrain design and delivery.

### Benefits and transition

Who owns outcomes after delivery and how value will be measured and sustained.

**NARRATOR**

Each element changes the others.

A scope increase may affect schedule, cost, resources, procurement, quality, risk, communications, and benefit timing. A supplier delay may change integration, staffing, test windows, cash flow, acceptance, and customer commitments.

## Scene 5. Assumptions are integration dependencies, 11:20 to 14:30

**VISUAL**

An assumption register connects to plans.

1. Specialist availability.
2. Regulatory review duration.
3. Supplier lead time.
4. Customer participation.
5. Data quality.
6. Funding timing.
7. Technology performance.
8. Benefit adoption.
9. Contract approval.
10. External market condition.

**NARRATOR**

Plans often conflict because assumptions are hidden or inconsistent.

An assumption should identify:

1. The statement being treated as true.
2. The source and owner.
3. The plans and decisions that depend on it.
4. The evidence that can confirm or challenge it.
5. The review date.
6. The risk if it is wrong.
7. The action when it changes.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

The schedule assumes a specialist is available in June. The resource plan assigns the specialist in August. The cost model assumes no external replacement. The integration plan cannot be credible until the assumption is resolved.

## Scene 6. Baselines, forecasts, and current reality, 14:30 to 18:10

**VISUAL**

Three project states appear.

### Approved baseline

The authorized reference for scope, schedule, cost, or another controlled commitment.

### Current forecast

The best evidence-based prediction of what will occur under current conditions.

### Actual performance

What has occurred, supported by source evidence.

**NARRATOR**

A baseline is not a forecast. A forecast is not an approval. Actual performance is not a plan.

The integrated plan should preserve all three.

When forecast differs from baseline, the project manager should analyze cause, impact, options, authority, and action. Hiding the forecast to protect a green status prevents responsible decisions.

**ADAPTIVE CONTEXT**

Adaptive projects may use product goals, release forecasts, capacity, flow measures, and rolling plans instead of a fully detailed long-range baseline. They still need an authorized commitment model and a current forecast.

**HYBRID CONTEXT**

Hybrid programs may maintain formal integrated milestones and cost commitments while adaptive teams manage detailed backlog and iteration plans.

## Scene 7. Evidence-based planning using schedule and cost guidance, 18:10 to 22:50

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
GAO Schedule Assessment Guide, GAO 16 89G  
GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide, GAO 20 195G

**NARRATOR**

GAO guidance describes integrated schedules and reliable cost estimates as tools for management and oversight.

A high-quality schedule should represent all activities needed to accomplish the work, logically sequence those activities, assign resources, establish reasonable durations, confirm the critical path, test uncertainty, update progress, and maintain a controlled baseline.

A reliable cost estimate should define purpose and scope, develop the technical baseline and work structure, collect data, establish estimating methods, include risk and uncertainty, document the estimate, present it for approval, and update it with actual costs and changes.

**NARRATOR**

These guides reinforce one integration principle: schedule, cost, scope, technical assumptions, risk, and work structure must describe the same program.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

GAO guidance was created for government acquisition and program oversight. It provides rigorous practices but does not require every commercial project to use identical artifacts or detail.

## Scene 8. Documented public case: portfolio plans and concentrated exposure, 22:50 to 25:40

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
NASA: Assessments of Major Projects  
GAO 25 107591  
Published 1 July 2025  
Source ID: GAO NASA MAJOR PROJECTS 2025

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that NASA planned about 74 billion dollars in major-project investment for fiscal year 2025. Four of 18 assessed projects had cost overruns and three had schedule delays. GAO's historical analysis reported that three of 53 projects accounted for almost half of total overruns.

This evidence illustrates why integrated planning should reveal concentration and cross-project consequences.

A portfolio average may appear stable while a small number of projects drive material cost, schedule, technical, or mission exposure. An integrated plan should make those dependencies and decision needs visible.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

The report does not establish one universal integration method or one cause for overruns. Technical, acquisition, funding, workforce, governance, and external conditions can all contribute.

## Scene 9. Original enterprise scenario: Meridian Global Transformation, 25:40 to 31:00

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Meridian is replacing finance, procurement, and workforce systems across 22 countries.

1. Scope assumes one global template with local exceptions.
2. The schedule assumes local requirements are approved before build.
3. Legal review is scheduled after build.
4. Procurement contracts the integrator for fixed milestones.
5. Data owners have not accepted cleansing responsibilities.
6. Finance assumes benefit realization when legacy systems retire.
7. Operations plans to keep legacy systems for one year.
8. The cost model excludes dual operation.
9. Communication announces a single global launch.
10. The rollout plan is phased.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Continue because each plan has an owner.  
B. Ask the scheduler to reconcile everything alone.  
C. Facilitate an integrated planning review, identify conflicting assumptions and dependencies, assign accountable decisions, and update the authorized plan and forecast.  
D. Choose the most conservative date and leave other plans unchanged.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. Integration requires cross-functional decisions and updates, not administrative merging.

**INTEGRATION REVIEW OUTPUT**

1. One global and local requirement decision model.
2. Legal and compliance reviews before dependent build decisions.
3. Data ownership and acceptance.
4. Contract change and supplier alignment.
5. Integrated rollout, legacy retirement, and benefit timing.
6. Dual-operation cost and resource forecast.
7. One stakeholder communication model.
8. One risk, issue, and decision record.

**FOLLOW UP**

The project changes from one global launch to three regional waves. The baseline is governed, contracts are updated, benefits are reforecast, local approvals are sequenced, and the executive report states the change and uncertainty.

## Scene 10. Maintaining the plan, 31:00 to 33:20

**VISUAL**

An integrated planning cadence appears.

1. Collect actual performance and new evidence.
2. Update schedule, cost, resources, risk, quality, procurement, and benefits.
3. Reconcile cross-plan assumptions.
4. Analyze variance and forecast.
5. Identify decisions and changes.
6. Obtain authorization.
7. Communicate and implement.
8. Verify and retain the decision record.

**NARRATOR**

The plan should be updated at a cadence appropriate to the work and when material events occur.

A plan that is not maintained becomes historical documentation. A plan updated without controlled authority becomes an ungoverned forecast. An integrated plan preserves both learning and accountability.

## Scene 11. Common PMP integration traps, 33:20 to 35:10

### Trap 1. Update only the schedule

A material change may also affect cost, resources, risk, procurement, quality, communications, and benefits.

### Trap 2. Treat subsidiary plans as independent

Identify assumptions and dependencies across them.

### Trap 3. Protect the baseline by hiding the forecast

Report the evidence and use change governance.

### Trap 4. Build one huge document no one uses

Tailor the format, but preserve integration and source authority.

### Trap 5. Let the project manager decide every conflict

Some integration decisions belong to sponsors, product owners, risk owners, control owners, or governance bodies.

### Trap 6. Integrate once at planning

Maintain integration throughout delivery, change, transition, and closure.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

The schedule assumes twelve months, while the contract requires delivery in eight months. What should the project manager do first?

A. Use the contract date and ignore the schedule.  
B. Facilitate an integrated review of assumptions, scope, resources, risk, contract terms, and authority.  
C. Ask the team to work faster.  
D. Remove the contract from the plan.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which statement best distinguishes a baseline from a forecast?

A. They are always identical.  
B. The baseline is an approved reference; the forecast is the current evidence-based prediction.  
C. A forecast requires no evidence.  
D. A baseline cannot change through governance.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A scope change is approved. What should the project manager do next?

A. Update only the scope statement.  
B. Update all affected plans, baselines, contracts, risks, communications, and benefit assumptions and verify implementation.  
C. Wait until closure.  
D. Tell the team verbally.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

What is the strongest purpose of an integrated project-management plan?

A. Create one large document.  
B. Connect project objectives, commitments, assumptions, decisions, plans, and evidence into a coherent management system.  
C. Eliminate change.  
D. Transfer all planning to the scheduler.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 35:10 to 36:20

**NARRATOR**

An integrated project-management plan is a coherent decision system. It connects purpose, scope, schedule, cost, resources, quality, risk, procurement, communications, compliance, benefits, governance, transition, and learning.

On the PMP exam, when plans conflict, do not update one artifact in isolation or ask one specialist to solve a governance problem. Identify the assumptions and dependencies, engage the accountable roles, analyze impact, govern the decision, update every affected element, and verify the result.

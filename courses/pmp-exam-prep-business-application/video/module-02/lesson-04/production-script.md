## Scene 3. Trust and psychological safety are related but different, 03:15 to 06:40

**VISUAL**

Two overlapping circles appear.

### Trust

A willingness to rely on another person, group, process, or institution based on expectations about competence, integrity, reliability, care, or behavior.

### Psychological safety

A shared team belief that interpersonal risk taking, such as asking for help, admitting uncertainty, reporting an error, or challenging an idea, can occur without unreasonable humiliation or punishment.

**NARRATOR**

Trust often concerns what one person expects from another. Psychological safety concerns the shared climate for speaking, learning, questioning, and admitting limits within the team.

A team can trust a highly capable leader but still fear challenging that leader. A team can be socially comfortable but avoid difficult accountability. A team can disagree vigorously and still maintain psychological safety when the challenge is respectful, evidence based, and focused on the work.

**ON SCREEN DISTINCTIONS**

Psychological safety is not:

1. Agreement.
2. Comfort at all times.
3. Low performance standards.
4. Freedom from consequences.
5. Permission to behave disrespectfully.
6. A guarantee that every idea will be adopted.
7. A substitute for competence, governance, or evidence.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Psychological safety supports candor. Accountability governs what the team does with that candor.

## Scene 4. What the research supports, 06:40 to 10:15

**VISUAL**

A research evidence ladder appears. Each study is identified by type, sample, context, finding, and limitation.

### Edmondson, 1999

**SOURCE CARD**

Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams  
Administrative Science Quarterly  
DOI 10.2307/2666999  
Source ID: EDMONDSON PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY 1999

**NARRATOR**

The 1999 study examined 51 work teams in a manufacturing company using a multimethod field design. Psychological safety was associated with team learning behavior, and the tested model found that learning behavior mediated the relationship between psychological safety and team performance. The paper also identified context support and team leader coaching as relevant structures in the model.

**LIMITATION**

This was a context specific organizational study. It does not establish that psychological safety alone causes high performance in every team.

### O'Donovan and McAuliffe, 2020

**SOURCE CARD**

A Systematic Review of Factors That Enable Psychological Safety in Healthcare Teams  
International Journal for Quality in Health Care  
Source ID: ODONOVAN MCAULIFFE PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY 2020

**NARRATOR**

The review included 36 studies published between 1999 and 2019. It identified 13 enablers grouped into broader themes involving safety and learning orientation, support, familiarity, hierarchy and status, inclusiveness, and individual differences.

**LIMITATION**

The review focused on healthcare teams. Project leaders should not assume every finding transfers unchanged to every industry.

### Montgomery and colleagues, 2025

**SOURCE CARD**

Psychological Safety and Patient Safety: A Systematic and Narrative Review  
PLOS ONE  
Source ID: MONTGOMERY PSYCHOLOGICAL PATIENT SAFETY 2025

**NARRATOR**

The review included nine papers with heterogeneous objective patient safety outcomes. Five reported a significant relationship between psychological safety and selected outcomes. The authors concluded that no clear overall causal conclusion could be extracted from the evidence.

**NARRATOR**

This limitation matters. A high number of reported problems may indicate a safer reporting culture, a higher underlying problem rate, or both. Project leaders must interpret data in context.

**ON SCREEN RESEARCH RULE**

> Use evidence to improve judgment. Do not convert association into certainty or one intervention into a universal cure.

## Scene 5. Diversity as a decision quality control, 10:15 to 13:30

**VISUAL**

A project decision is analyzed by several perspectives:

1. Customer.
2. Operations.
3. Security.
4. Finance.
5. Legal.
6. Engineering.
7. Accessibility.
8. Geography.
9. Frontline workforce.
10. Executive strategy.

Each perspective reveals a different consequence.

**NARRATOR**

Diversity can include professional discipline, experience, cognitive approach, geography, culture, identity, organizational position, risk exposure, and stakeholder impact.

Diversity creates potential information. It does not automatically create better decisions.

The potential is lost when:

1. High status voices dominate.
2. Minority perspectives are invited but ignored.
3. Meetings reward speed over inquiry.
4. dissent is treated as disloyalty.
5. Technical language excludes affected stakeholders.
6. The team gathers perspectives but uses no decision criteria.
7. The final record conceals disagreement.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A global authentication change appears technically sound. The accessibility specialist identifies barriers for users with certain disabilities. Regional legal teams identify different identity proofing constraints. Frontline support predicts a surge in account recovery. The diverse evidence improves the decision only if the project system receives and acts on it.

## Scene 6. The leader's operating practices, 13:30 to 17:20

**VISUAL**

Six leadership practices appear in an executive team environment.

### Frame the work as uncertain and interdependent

The leader states what is known, what is uncertain, and why expertise from multiple roles is needed.

### Model fallibility and learning

The leader acknowledges limits, asks for correction, and changes position when evidence changes.

### Invite specific participation

Instead of asking “Any concerns?”, the leader asks:

1. What could make this decision wrong?
2. Which assumption has the weakest evidence?
3. What are we not seeing from the customer or operational perspective?
4. Who has information that has not entered the decision?
5. Which risk would be difficult to raise in this room?

### Respond productively

The leader thanks the person, clarifies the evidence, protects the issue from retaliation, and separates the messenger from the problem.

### Establish a fair response process

Concerns are assessed using visible criteria and assigned an owner, threshold, and resolution path.

### Close the learning loop

The team sees what happened after it spoke up. Unresolved concerns do not disappear into a meeting record.

**NARRATOR**

If people repeatedly raise concerns that receive no response, requests to speak up will lose credibility.

## Scene 7. Accountability and respectful challenge, 17:20 to 19:45

**VISUAL**

A two axis model appears.

Horizontal axis: psychological safety.  
Vertical axis: performance accountability.

Four environments form:

1. Low safety, low accountability: avoidance and disengagement.
2. High safety, low accountability: comfort without disciplined delivery.
3. Low safety, high accountability: anxiety, concealment, and compliance behavior.
4. High safety, high accountability: candid learning and responsible performance.

**NARRATOR**

The target is not comfort. The target is a team that can surface reality and still meet professional standards.

Respectful challenge focuses on evidence, assumptions, decisions, impacts, and standards. It does not attack identity, motive, or dignity.

Accountability requires:

1. Clear expectations.
2. Competence and resources.
3. Evidence based feedback.
4. Fair process.
5. Proportionate consequences.
6. Learning after error.
7. Escalation for willful misconduct, safety violations, or material risk.

## Scene 8. Documented public case: Challenger decision process, 19:45 to 24:10

**VISUAL**

A respectful, source labeled documentary sequence. The crew is not dramatized. No accident imagery is used for spectacle.

**SOURCE CARD**

Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident, Volume 1  
Published 6 June 1986  
Source ID: NASA ROGERS COMMISSION 1986

**NARRATOR**

The Rogers Commission examined the technical causes of the Challenger accident and the chain of decisions that culminated in approval of the launch.

The Commission identified failures in communication that resulted in a launch decision based on incomplete and misleading information. It identified conflict between engineering data and management judgments. It also identified a management structure that permitted flight safety problems to bypass key Shuttle managers.

**VISUAL**

A decision chain shows how technical concern, management judgment, communication, authority, and schedule pressure interacted.

**NARRATOR**

This case demonstrates why project teams need more than technical expertise. They need a system in which evidence, dissent, uncertainty, and safety concerns reach the accountable decision makers without distortion.

**LIMITATIONS AND RESPECT CARD**

1. The Commission did not use psychological safety as its formal causal label.
2. The accident had technical, design, testing, communication, management, and organizational contributors.
3. This lesson must not reduce a fatal accident to a simple workplace metaphor.
4. The case is used to teach documented decision and communication failures respectfully.
5. Learners should consult the official Commission report before professional use.

**APPLICATION QUESTION**

> Which project control ensures that a technical concern reaches the authorized decision owner with its evidence, dissent, and limitations intact?

Learners compare an informal meeting, a documented risk escalation path, an anonymous survey, and a general open door policy. Feedback explains why culture and formal governance must work together.

## Scene 9. Original enterprise scenario: Meridian Medical Launch, 24:10 to 28:30

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Meridian is preparing a software enabled medical product launch.

1. The project dashboard is green.
2. A junior validation engineer believes one test condition was excluded.
3. The engineering director says the issue is unlikely and schedule recovery is impossible.
4. The project manager asks whether the engineer is “certain enough to stop the launch.”
5. The engineer withdraws the concern because the evidence is incomplete.
6. The team has no defined process for recording unresolved technical dissent.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Continue because the engineer cannot prove failure.  
B. Ask the engineer to discuss the matter privately after launch.  
C. Record the concern, clarify the evidence and uncertainty, engage the appropriate technical and safety authorities, and route the decision through the authorized review process.  
D. Cancel the launch immediately without further analysis.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. A concern does not need perfect proof before it enters risk analysis. The project manager should protect the evidence path, respect specialist authority, and ensure an accountable decision.

A treats uncertainty as absence of risk. B delays the evidence and isolates the concern. D assumes authority and outcome before analysis.

**FOLLOW UP**

The review finds that the excluded condition is not safety critical but reveals a documentation gap. The project proceeds with an approved evidence correction and a new prelaunch verification control.

Psychological safety did not require automatic cancellation. It enabled the project to receive the concern, assess it, decide, and learn.

## Scene 11. Common PMP team traps, 30:40 to 32:25

### Trap 1. Avoid conflict to protect harmony

Constructive disagreement can improve decisions. The project manager should facilitate respectful evidence based resolution.

### Trap 2. Escalate every disagreement

Teams should resolve issues within their authority when possible. Escalate when authority, ethics, risk, or unresolved barriers require it.

### Trap 3. Assume silence means agreement

Verify understanding and invite specific challenge.

### Trap 4. Treat psychological safety as low standards

Psychological safety and accountability should reinforce each other.

### Trap 5. Add diverse participants without changing the decision process

Representation alone does not guarantee that information influences the decision.

### Trap 6. Interpret higher reporting as proof that performance is worsening

Reporting may increase because trust improves, because incidents increase, or both. Analyze context and outcomes.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A junior specialist raises an incomplete but potentially material concern. What should the project manager do first?

A. Require definitive proof before recording it.  
B. Record and clarify the concern, protect the evidence path, and engage the appropriate authority.  
C. Ask the specialist to wait until the next retrospective.  
D. Decide personally whether the concern is valid.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which statement best describes psychological safety?

A. The team never experiences conflict.  
B. Team members can take interpersonal risks such as asking questions or admitting uncertainty without unreasonable humiliation or punishment.  
C. Every idea must be approved.  
D. Performance consequences are prohibited.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A project reports more defects after leaders encourage early reporting. What should the project manager conclude?

A. Quality definitely worsened.  
B. Psychological safety definitely improved.  
C. The increase requires contextual analysis of reporting behavior, actual defect rates, detection timing, and outcomes.  
D. The metric should be removed.

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 4

Which environment best supports high performance?

A. High psychological safety and clear accountability.  
B. High psychological safety and no standards.  
C. Low psychological safety and strict deadlines.  
D. Low psychological safety and low accountability.

**Correct response:** A.

## Closing summary, 32:25 to 33:35

**NARRATOR**

High performing project teams need access to reality. That requires diverse evidence, trust, respectful dissent, psychological safety, clear accountability, and a governance system that responds when people speak.

Psychological safety does not eliminate standards or difficult decisions. It allows the team to surface what must be managed.

On the PMP exam, look for hidden information, hierarchy, suppressed risk, unfair process, or missing learning loops. Invite evidence, engage the right roles, preserve accountability, act through authority, and verify what changes.

**ON SCREEN**

> Make it safe to surface reality. Make it necessary to act responsibly.

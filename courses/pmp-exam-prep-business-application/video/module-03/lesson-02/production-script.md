## Scene 1. Cold open: the project that met the requirement and disappointed the customer, 00:09 to 02:25

**VISUAL**

A customer portal project reaches acceptance.

The contract requirement reads:

> Customers can submit service requests online.

The feature passes testing. The project reports completion.

After launch:

1. Customers can submit requests but cannot see status.
2. Mobile users abandon the process at a high rate.
3. Support staff reenter information manually.
4. Customers expected a two day response, but operations planned five days.
5. The sponsor expected call volume to fall, but the support center receives more calls.
6. The product team says the customer requested only online submission.

**NARRATOR**

The requirement was delivered. The expectations were not aligned.

Stakeholder expectation management is not promising more. It is creating an accurate, shared, and governed understanding of outcomes, scope, quality, timing, responsibilities, risk, and uncertainty.

## Scene 3. Where expectations come from, 03:10 to 06:40

**VISUAL**

Expectations emerge from eight sources.

1. Contracts and formal requirements.
2. Strategy and business case.
3. Prior experience.
4. Market promises and sales messages.
5. Stakeholder assumptions.
6. Cultural norms and professional standards.
7. Regulation, policy, and control obligations.
8. Informal statements by leaders or team members.

**NARRATOR**

Some expectations are explicit. Others are inferred.

A customer may believe that “real time” means seconds while the technical team means fifteen minutes. An executive may believe that “global deployment” means every business unit on one date while the project plan means a phased rollout. A user may believe that a new system will preserve local workflow while the sponsor expects standardization.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> An expectation can influence trust and satisfaction even when it is not an approved requirement.

**NARRATOR**

The project manager should not automatically convert every expectation into scope. The project manager should identify it, understand its source and importance, assess the impact, and route required decisions through governance.

## Scene 4. Expectation evidence record, 06:40 to 10:00

**VISUAL**

An expectation record appears with ten fields.

1. Stakeholder or customer.
2. Expected outcome or experience.
3. Source of expectation.
4. Whether it is an approved requirement, assumption, preference, promise, or obligation.
5. Measure and target.
6. Delivery or transition owner.
7. Timing.
8. Dependencies and risk.
9. Decision authority and change path.
10. Communication and verification method.

**NARRATOR**

This record allows the project to separate five questions:

1. What does the stakeholder expect?
2. What has the organization actually committed to?
3. What evidence supports the expectation?
4. What would be required to change the commitment?
5. How will the stakeholder know whether the outcome occurred?

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A finance executive expects a new platform to reduce operating cost by 20 percent in the first year. The approved business case assumes 20 percent only after three years and after retirement of two legacy systems.

The project manager should surface the mismatch before launch, not after the first year benefit review.

## Scene 5. Aligning on outcomes and success measures, 10:00 to 13:20

**VISUAL**

A success alignment canvas appears.

### Purpose

Why the project exists.

### Outcomes

What should change for stakeholders or operations.

### Measures

What evidence will demonstrate the change.

### Boundaries

What the project includes and excludes.

### Responsibilities

Who delivers, adopts, operates, measures, and owns benefits.

### Trade offs

Which priorities may conflict and who decides.

### Uncertainty

Which assumptions or external conditions may change the result.

**NARRATOR**

Alignment does not require every stakeholder to prefer the same outcome. It requires a clear, authorized decision about which outcomes the project will pursue and how success will be measured.

When expectations conflict, the project manager should facilitate evidence based discussion and identify the accountable decision owner.

**DECISION EXAMPLE**

Customers want more functionality. Security wants lower data exposure. Operations wants standardization. Sales wants customization. Finance wants reduced cost.

The project manager should not select a compromise alone. The manager should clarify value, risk, constraints, options, impact, and authority so the organization can make a governed decision.

## Scene 6. Satisfaction, value, and expectation gaps, 13:20 to 16:20

**VISUAL**

Three measures appear.

### Requirement conformance

Did the output meet the approved specification?

### Outcome realization

Did the intended operational or stakeholder change occur?

### Stakeholder experience and satisfaction

How did stakeholders experience the product, process, communication, support, and transition?

**NARRATOR**

These measures are related but not identical.

A system can conform to requirements and still create poor user experience. Stakeholders can be satisfied with a project that does not achieve strategic value. Satisfaction can be low during a necessary change that produces long term value.

The project manager should interpret satisfaction alongside outcomes, requirements, adoption, risk, and evidence.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Do not manage expectations by lowering truth. Manage them by clarifying reality, authority, options, and consequences.

## Scene 7. Documented public case: VA deployment pause and incremental improvement, 16:20 to 21:10

**VISUAL**

A source labeled government modernization case appears.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
Electronic Health Records: VA Making Incremental Improvements in New System but Needs Updated Cost Estimate and Schedule  
GAO 25 106874  
Published 12 March 2025  
Source ID: GAO VA EHR INCREMENTAL IMPROVEMENT 2025

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that the Department of Veterans Affairs paused deployments of its modernized electronic health record in 2023 because of user concerns.

During the pause, GAO reported incremental configuration and system improvements. VA worked on configuration changes, selected operational improvements, support, and system performance. GAO also reported that additional complex projects required significant stakeholder coordination and collaboration with live sites.

GAO further reported that the program lacked an updated life cycle cost estimate and integrated schedule reflecting the changes and delays.

**NARRATOR**

This case shows why expectation management must remain connected to evidence and governance.

1. User experience can change deployment expectations.
2. A pause changes schedule, cost, benefit, and stakeholder assumptions.
3. Incremental improvements require clear criteria for readiness and value.
4. Stakeholder coordination must translate concerns into prioritized work and verified outcomes.
5. Updated cost and schedule evidence is necessary for realistic executive expectations.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

1. Federal healthcare modernization context.
2. The program involves many technical, clinical, contractual, governance, and organizational factors.
3. The case does not prove that stakeholder dissatisfaction alone caused the pause.
4. Incremental changes do not automatically prove readiness or value.
5. Time sensitive facts must be reverified before final production.

## Scene 8. Managing change without uncontrolled commitment, 21:10 to 24:10

**VISUAL**

A stakeholder request enters a governed change path.

1. Clarify the requested outcome.
2. Identify whether it is correction, clarification, requirement, preference, or new scope.
3. Analyze value, cost, schedule, risk, quality, compliance, and dependencies.
4. Identify decision authority.
5. Approve, reject, defer, or modify.
6. Update plans, baselines, backlog, contracts, and communications.
7. Verify the result.

**NARRATOR**

The project manager should acknowledge the expectation without promising the outcome before analysis and authorization.

**STRONG RESPONSE**

“I understand that same day status visibility is important. The approved release currently provides next day updates. I will document the requested outcome, assess value and impact with the team, and bring the decision to the authorized product and governance owners.”

**WEAK RESPONSE**

“We will add it.”

**NARRATOR**

Trust is damaged when the project makes commitments it cannot authorize or deliver.

## Scene 9. Expectation monitoring and feedback, 24:10 to 26:45

**VISUAL**

An expectation monitoring system appears.

1. Stakeholder feedback.
2. Adoption and usage.
3. Satisfaction measures.
4. Support demand.
5. Complaints and exceptions.
6. Benefit measures.
7. Requirement changes.
8. Change requests.
9. Training and readiness evidence.
10. Decision and communication history.

**NARRATOR**

Expectation alignment is not a one time workshop. It should be monitored as evidence and stakeholder needs change.

The project manager should ask:

1. Are stakeholders receiving the information they need?
2. Do they understand the current commitment and uncertainty?
3. Are expectations changing because the environment changed or because the project communicated poorly?
4. Which gaps require correction, support, change, or governance?
5. Are affected stakeholders experiencing consequences that were not anticipated?

## Scene 10. Original enterprise scenario: Horizon Customer Platform, 26:45 to 30:10

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Horizon is replacing a customer service platform.

1. The contract requires online request submission and status updates within one business day.
2. Sales leaders promised customers “real time visibility.”
3. Operations planned a five day service response.
4. Customers expect a two day response based on the legacy service agreement.
5. The product owner believes status visibility and response time are separate.
6. The launch is six weeks away.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Instruct the team to deliver every stakeholder expectation.  
B. Ignore the sales promise because it is not in the technical requirements.  
C. Facilitate alignment on the committed customer experience, verify contractual and business obligations, analyze gaps and options, and route changes through authorized governance.  
D. Delay launch indefinitely.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The project needs one authorized expectation model before launch.

A creates uncontrolled commitment. B ignores a real trust and commercial issue. D makes a schedule decision without analysis.

**FOLLOW UP**

Governance approves fifteen minute status updates, preserves the one business day status commitment, clarifies that service resolution targets vary by request type, and requires customer communication before launch.

The project updates requirements, service measures, training, support scripts, contract interpretation, and launch communications.

## Scene 11. Common PMP expectation traps, 30:10 to 32:10

### Trap 1. Say yes to protect satisfaction

Acknowledge the need, analyze impact, and obtain authorization before committing.

### Trap 2. Treat the approved scope as the only expectation

Understand assumptions, experience, outcomes, and promises that affect trust and value.

### Trap 3. Lower expectations by hiding risk

Communicate facts, uncertainty, consequences, and options honestly.

### Trap 4. Assume satisfaction equals success

Interpret satisfaction with outcome, value, quality, adoption, risk, and requirement evidence.

### Trap 5. Wait until the end to measure expectations

Monitor throughout delivery and transition.

### Trap 6. Treat every new expectation as scope creep

Some expectations reveal defects, omissions, legal obligations, or changes in the environment. Analyze before classifying.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A stakeholder expects a feature that is not in the approved scope but may provide significant value. What should the project manager do?

A. Promise the feature.  
B. Reject it immediately as scope creep.  
C. Clarify the expected outcome, analyze impact and value, and route the decision through change governance.  
D. Ask the team to add it without documentation.

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 2

A system meets requirements but user satisfaction is low. What should the project manager do?

A. Declare success and close all concerns.  
B. Analyze user experience, outcome, adoption, support, quality, and expectation evidence before recommending action.  
C. Remove satisfaction from reporting.  
D. Assume users resist all change.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

Which response best protects trust when a customer requests a major change?

A. “We will deliver it.”  
B. “That is not our problem.”  
C. “We will document the requested outcome, assess value and impact, and confirm the authorized decision and next steps.”  
D. “The current plan cannot change.”

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 4

Why should cost and schedule evidence be updated after a major deployment pause?

A. To make the dashboard more complex.  
B. To align stakeholder expectations and governance decisions with the changed project reality.  
C. To eliminate stakeholder feedback.  
D. To guarantee benefit realization.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 32:10 to 33:20

**NARRATOR**

Expectation management creates a shared and governed understanding of what the project will deliver, which outcomes matter, what remains uncertain, who owns the decision, and how success will be verified.

Do not protect satisfaction through unsupported promises or hidden risk. Surface expectations, align evidence and authority, analyze change, communicate honestly, and monitor stakeholder experience through transition.

On the PMP exam, when expectations change or conflict, do not automatically accept or reject them. Clarify, analyze, engage, govern, communicate, and verify.

**ON SCREEN**

> Align the outcome. Govern the promise. Measure the experience.

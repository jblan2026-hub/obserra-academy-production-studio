## Cold open: the decision everyone approved and no one owned

A steering committee verbally supports a high-risk release. The minutes say “approved by consensus.” No accountable decision owner is named, no residual-risk acceptance is documented, and no one can explain which authority permitted the release.

**NARRATOR**

Participation is not authority. Consensus is not necessarily accountability. Governance defines how project decisions are made, evidenced, challenged, approved, implemented, and reviewed.

## Learning objectives

2. Establish decision rights, roles, forums, thresholds, and escalation.

5. Govern predictive, adaptive, and hybrid work.

## Governance architecture

A project governance model identifies:

1. Purpose and strategic alignment.
2. Sponsor and accountable executive.
3. Project manager authority.
4. Product and benefit ownership.
5. Risk, control, legal, financial, and operational decision owners.
6. Governance bodies and delegated authority.
7. Decision categories and thresholds.
8. Evidence and quorum requirements.
9. Escalation and exception paths.
10. Reporting and assurance.
11. Audit and records.
12. Review and improvement cadence.

## Decision taxonomy

Projects should clarify authority for:

1. Charter and funding.
2. Scope and product priority.
3. Baseline and milestone changes.
4. Contract commitments.
5. Risk acceptance.
6. Legal and regulatory interpretation.
7. Security, safety, privacy, and quality exceptions.
8. Release and operational acceptance.
9. Benefit and value decisions.
10. Pause, termination, and restart.

**NARRATOR**

The project manager integrates the decision process but does not automatically own every decision.

## Governance proportionality

Governance should become stronger as consequence, uncertainty, external obligation, complexity, and stakeholder impact increase.

Too little governance can create unowned risk and unauthorized action. Too much governance can delay decisions, suppress team autonomy, and convert leadership into administrative approval.

A proportional model defines:

1. Decisions delegated to teams.
2. Decisions reserved to product or project leadership.
3. Decisions requiring specialist assurance.
4. Decisions requiring executive or board approval.
5. Emergency authority and retrospective review.

## Adaptive and hybrid governance

Adaptive delivery still requires purpose, funding, risk, product authority, quality, security, and release governance. The model may delegate backlog and implementation decisions while preserving thresholds for material scope, risk, cost, or obligation.

Hybrid governance should connect local team decisions to shared milestones, interfaces, risks, configuration, evidence, and enterprise outcomes.

## Governance evidence

A decision record should include:

1. Question and context.
2. Accountable owner and authority.
3. Participants and conflicts of interest.
4. Evidence, source, freshness, and confidence.
5. Options and trade-offs.
6. Decision and rationale.
7. Conditions and residual risk.
8. Implementation owner.
9. Verification and review date.
10. Dissent or unresolved limitation.

## Guidance-based example: integrated oversight

GAO schedule and cost guidance emphasizes integrated evidence, documented assumptions, approval, and maintenance. The broader governance lesson is that decision owners need one coherent view of technical, schedule, cost, risk, and uncertainty rather than isolated reports.

**Limitations:** Government program guidance does not prescribe one corporate governance body or charter.

## Original scenario: Polaris Release Authority

Polaris is preparing a global release.

1. The product owner supports release.
2. Security reports residual privileged-access risk.
3. Legal identifies one unresolved regional obligation.
4. The steering committee has advisory authority only.
5. The charter does not name the release-risk authority.
6. The sponsor asks the project manager to “own the decision.”

**Strongest action:** Clarify the decision rights and controlling policies, assemble required evidence, identify the authorized risk, legal, product, and executive owners, and expedite a governed release decision. Do not invent authority.

The organization establishes an interim release authority, records the decision and conditions, assigns remediation and verification, and updates the permanent governance model.

## Governance effectiveness measures

1. Decision cycle time.
2. Percentage of decisions with named authority and evidence.
3. Overdue decisions and impact.
4. Unapproved exceptions.
5. Reopened decisions due to missing evidence.
6. Team autonomy within delegated boundaries.
7. Stakeholder trust and transparency.
8. Audit and assurance findings.
9. Benefit and risk outcomes.
10. Governance actions completed.

## Common PMP traps

1. Escalate every issue to the sponsor.
2. Assume a committee owns decisions collectively without named accountability.
3. Allow governance to approve without sufficient evidence.
4. Treat adaptive delivery as governance-free.
5. Let the project manager accept enterprise risk without authority.
6. Keep a broken governance process because it was approved at kickoff.

## Knowledge checks

1. A decision exceeds delegated authority. **Escalate with evidence to the authorized owner.**
2. A committee advises but cannot approve. **Do not represent its recommendation as final authorization.**
3. Governance is delaying routine team decisions. **Review and delegate appropriate thresholds.**
4. A high-impact exception is requested. **Use the authorized exception and risk-acceptance process.**

## Scene 3. Define the gap before explaining it, 03:05 to 06:15

**VISUAL**

A performance statement is rewritten in three stages.

### Weak statement

“The team is underperforming.”

### Better statement

“The workstream missed the approved integration milestone by six weeks.”

### Decision useful statement

“The workstream missed the approved integration milestone by six weeks. The current forecast affects system testing, customer readiness, and contract acceptance. Two resource changes, one vendor delay, three unresolved requirement decisions, and repeated quality rework are documented. Individual and system contributions require analysis.”

**NARRATOR**

A performance problem should be defined using observable evidence, agreed expectations, impact, timing, and uncertainty.

Avoid labels such as lazy, resistant, difficult, weak, or not committed unless a governed process has established specific behavior and relevance. Labels create bias and conceal the evidence needed for action.

**ON SCREEN PERFORMANCE GAP MODEL**

> Expected result minus observed result, interpreted through context, evidence, and impact.

**NARRATOR**

The project manager should ask:

1. What was expected?
2. Was the expectation clear, realistic, and authorized?
3. What occurred?
4. What evidence supports the conclusion?
5. What changed in the environment?
6. What is the project impact?
7. What remains unknown?

## Scene 4. The eight cause categories, 06:15 to 10:30

**VISUAL**

Eight diagnostic pathways form around the performance gap.

### 1. Clarity

The objective, role, acceptance criteria, priority, or authority is unclear.

### 2. Capability

The person or team lacks required knowledge, skill, experience, or practice.

### 3. Capacity

Time, staffing, workload, tools, or attention are insufficient.

### 4. Process and system

Workflow, governance, technology, data, quality controls, or organizational design creates the problem.

### 5. Dependency

Another team, supplier, approval, decision, or external event blocks performance.

### 6. Incentive and priority

Measures or leadership signals reward behavior that conflicts with project goals.

### 7. Commitment or behavior

A person or team understands the expectation and has the ability and resources but does not follow through or engages in harmful conduct.

### 8. Change and uncertainty

The original plan no longer fits the facts, and the apparent performance gap may reflect a needed project decision rather than poor execution.

**NARRATOR**

Several causes may coexist. A coaching intervention cannot solve an absent resource. More training cannot solve unclear authority. A process redesign cannot excuse deliberate misconduct.

**INTERACTIVE DIAGNOSTIC**

Learners receive six project symptoms and identify the most likely cause categories and the evidence needed before action.

## Scene 5. Structured root cause analysis, 10:30 to 13:30

**VISUAL**

A structured analysis sequence replaces a rapid blame decision.

1. Define the problem.
2. Preserve facts and timing.
3. Map the process and dependencies.
4. Identify changes and decision delays.
5. Gather perspectives from affected roles.
6. Test plausible causes.
7. Distinguish contributing causes from root causes.
8. Select interventions within authority.
9. Assign owners and measures.
10. Verify results and update lessons.

**NARRATOR**

Root cause analysis is not a ritual of asking “why” until one answer appears. Complex project problems often have multiple interacting causes.

A useful analysis asks whether removing or changing a cause would materially reduce recurrence. It also identifies controls that failed to detect or contain the problem.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

Repeated defects appear in a data migration.

Possible causes include changing source definitions, inadequate test data, rushed review, missing business ownership, an overloaded specialist, poor automation, and unclear acceptance criteria.

The project manager should not assume that the engineer who found or entered the defect caused the system problem.

## Scene 6. Coaching conversation architecture, 13:30 to 17:00

**VISUAL**

A private coaching conversation appears with six stages.

### Stage 1. Establish purpose

“This conversation is to understand the delivery gap and agree on the support and actions needed to restore performance.”

### Stage 2. Present observable evidence

State dates, commitments, outputs, impacts, and prior decisions without exaggeration.

### Stage 3. Invite the other perspective

Ask what occurred, which barriers exist, what assumptions differ, and what support is needed.

### Stage 4. Diagnose together

Separate capability, capacity, clarity, dependency, system, and behavior factors.

### Stage 5. Agree on action

Define the expected result, support, owner, timing, evidence, checkpoints, and escalation condition.

### Stage 6. Follow through

Review progress, recognize improvement, adjust support, and escalate if agreed actions are not completed or risk increases.

**NARRATOR**

Coaching should preserve dignity and accountability. Avoid public humiliation, vague criticism, unsupported conclusions, and promises the project manager cannot make.

**ETHICS CONNECTION**

Responsibility requires accurate evidence and follow through. Respect requires direct, private, and professional communication. Fairness requires consistent criteria and consideration of context. Honesty requires clear feedback about impact and expectations.

## Scene 7. Intervention selection, 17:00 to 20:00

**VISUAL**

A diagnostic to intervention map appears.

### If clarity is weak

Clarify purpose, role, priority, criteria, and decision authority.

### If capability is weak

Provide training, mentoring, practice, expert support, or a staged assignment.

### If capacity is weak

Reprioritize, add resources, change timing, reduce scope through governance, or remove competing work.

### If process is weak

Redesign workflow, automate controls, improve data, strengthen review, or change governance.

### If a dependency is blocking

Assign ownership, negotiate commitments, escalate through the proper path, or change the plan.

### If incentives conflict

Engage leadership to align measures, rewards, and priorities.

### If behavior or conduct is the issue

Use clear expectations, documented feedback, organizational policy, and appropriate human resources or management processes.

### If uncertainty invalidates the plan

Reassess assumptions, options, risk, and the authorized baseline.

**NARRATOR**

The intervention should match the cause. Generic pressure often increases hidden defects, burnout, and reporting delay without solving the system problem.

## Scene 8. Documented public case: planning and design failure, 20:00 to 23:25

**VISUAL**

A source labeled historical project case appears using original Obserra graphics.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
Space Station: Inadequate Planning and Design Led to Propulsion Module Project Failure  
GAO 01 633  
Published 20 June 2001  
Source ID: GAO PROPULSION MODULE 2001

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that an initial propulsion module project did not meet performance, cost, and schedule goals largely because fundamental planning and execution processes were not followed. A subsequent assessment defined success criteria, identified design assumptions, and compared alternatives before recommending a design.

The case demonstrates why leaders should examine the project system before attributing failure to individual effort.

A weak problem definition, incomplete success criteria, hidden assumptions, and insufficient alternatives analysis can create poor performance even when people work hard.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

1. Historical government project context.
2. The report does not evaluate every individual contributor.
3. The case does not prove that one planning technique guarantees success.
4. Technical, funding, organizational, and external conditions also matter.
5. Learners should review the official report before professional use.

**APPLICATION QUESTION**

> Which intervention is strongest when a project repeatedly misses targets because success criteria and design assumptions were never established?

Learners compare additional overtime, individual discipline, clearer criteria and alternatives analysis, and immediate closure.

## Scene 9. Original scenario: Sentinel Data Migration, 23:25 to 27:55

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Sentinel Financial Services is migrating customer data to a new platform.

1. The migration lead has missed two test milestones.
2. The project manager believes the lead is not sufficiently committed.
3. Source data definitions changed after planning.
4. Two analysts were reassigned.
5. The vendor mapping tool produces inconsistent results.
6. Business owners have not approved exception rules.
7. The lead has worked repeated weekends and stopped reporting new risks.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do first?

**OPTIONS**

A. Begin formal discipline because two milestones were missed.  
B. Replace the lead immediately.  
C. Define the performance gap, analyze system and individual causes with the lead and relevant owners, and agree on an evidence based intervention plan.  
D. Add another deadline without changing resources or decisions.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The project manager needs evidence before deciding whether the problem is capability, capacity, process, dependency, behavior, or a combination.

A and B may become appropriate only after evidence and organizational process support them. D increases pressure without addressing cause.

**FOLLOW UP**

The analysis shows that the lead failed to escalate one dependency promptly, but the primary drivers are data changes, missing business decisions, and lost capacity.

The intervention includes:

1. Clarified escalation expectations.
2. A business decision deadline.
3. Restored analytical capacity.
4. Vendor defect containment.
5. Revised test sequencing.
6. Twice weekly evidence checkpoints.
7. A recovery decision through governance.

The coaching addresses the individual's escalation gap while the project system addresses the larger causes.

## Scene 10. When to escalate beyond coaching, 27:55 to 29:50

**VISUAL**

Escalation thresholds appear.

1. Safety or security risk.
2. Legal, regulatory, ethical, or contractual concern.
3. Harassment, discrimination, retaliation, fraud, or misconduct.
4. Material risk outside project authority.
5. Repeated failure after clear support and expectations.
6. Lack of required competence for high consequence work.
7. Resource or priority conflict requiring management authority.
8. A systemic barrier the team cannot remove.

**NARRATOR**

Coaching is not a substitute for required escalation or formal organizational process.

The project manager should preserve facts, protect confidentiality, avoid unauthorized investigation, and engage the appropriate manager, human resources, legal, security, compliance, safety, or governance role.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Coach what is coachable. Govern what requires authority. Escalate what exceeds the project boundary.

## Scene 11. Common PMP performance traps, 29:50 to 31:35

### Trap 1. Replace the team member first

First understand the cause unless immediate safety, misconduct, or risk requires removal.

### Trap 2. Escalate before coaching

When the issue is within project authority and safe to address, coach and support before escalating.

### Trap 3. Coach a structural barrier

An individual cannot solve missing authority, chronic understaffing, or an unresolved sponsor decision alone.

### Trap 4. Avoid direct feedback to protect morale

Respectful, specific feedback supports fairness and improvement. Silence can prolong harm.

### Trap 5. Add pressure to recover schedule

Pressure without cause analysis may create more defects and concealment.

### Trap 6. Treat every error as misconduct

Differentiate learning, capability, negligence, willful behavior, and system failure through evidence and policy.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A workstream misses a milestone after two specialists are reassigned and a dependency is delayed. What should the project manager do first?

A. Discipline the workstream lead.  
B. Define the gap and analyze capacity, dependency, process, and individual factors.  
C. Add overtime.  
D. Remove the milestone from the plan.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which coaching feedback is strongest?

A. “You are not committed.”  
B. “The approved risk report was due Friday and was not submitted. The governance meeting lacked the evidence needed for a decision. Let us understand what blocked completion and agree on a recovery plan.”  
C. “Everyone is disappointed in you.”  
D. “Do better next time.”

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A performance problem is caused by unclear acceptance criteria. Which intervention is most appropriate?

A. Additional discipline.  
B. Clarify and agree on measurable acceptance criteria, roles, and verification.  
C. Replace the entire team.  
D. Stop reporting the problem.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

When should a project manager move beyond ordinary coaching?

A. Whenever a team member disagrees.  
B. When the issue involves material risk, misconduct, legal or ethical concerns, repeated failure after support, or authority beyond the project manager.  
C. When a task takes longer than estimated.  
D. When the sponsor asks for more detail.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 31:35 to 32:45

**NARRATOR**

Performance problems are evidence problems before they are intervention problems.

Define the gap. Diagnose the causes. Separate system conditions from individual behavior. Match support and accountability to the evidence. Escalate when risk, conduct, authority, or organizational policy requires it. Verify whether the intervention improves the project result.

On the PMP exam, avoid reflexive blame, replacement, or escalation. First understand what is happening and choose the strongest proportionate next action.

**ON SCREEN**

> Define. Diagnose. Coach. Govern. Verify.

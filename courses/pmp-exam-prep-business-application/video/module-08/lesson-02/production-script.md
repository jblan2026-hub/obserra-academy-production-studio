# Part I. Continuous improvement

## Improvement loop

1. Observe performance and outcomes.
2. Capture feedback, incidents, defects, delays, and lessons.
3. Analyze causes and context.
4. Prioritize improvement by value and risk.
5. Assign owner and authority.
6. Implement change.
7. Measure effectiveness.
8. Standardize, adapt, or reverse.
9. Share applicable learning.
10. Reassess as conditions change.

Retrospectives and lessons learned create value only when they alter future decisions, processes, controls, or behavior.

## Improvement evidence

An improvement record should include:

1. Problem or opportunity.
2. Evidence and baseline.
3. Root cause or hypothesis.
4. Proposed action.
5. Owner and due date.
6. Expected measure.
7. Risk and side effects.
8. Implementation status.
9. Result and confidence.
10. Transfer to organizational process assets.

**EXAM TRAP**

Recording the same lesson repeatedly is not continuous improvement. Assign action, change the system, and verify.

# Part II. Organizational change and adoption

## Change-readiness model

1. Clear reason and strategic purpose.
2. Visible sponsor commitment.
3. Stakeholder impact analysis.
4. Readiness and capability assessment.
5. Communication and participation.
6. Training and practice.
7. Process, policy, role, and incentive alignment.
8. Support and transition.
9. Adoption and outcome measures.
10. Reinforcement and corrective action.

**NARRATOR**

Resistance can reflect loss, risk, poor communication, workload, identity, weak evidence, conflicting incentives, or legitimate defects. Do not label every objection as unwillingness to change.

## Adoption measures

1. Usage by intended population.
2. Task completion and error rate.
3. Process conformance.
4. Workarounds.
5. Support demand.
6. Training-to-performance transfer.
7. Satisfaction and trust.
8. Benefit realization.
9. Manager reinforcement.
10. Distribution across regions and stakeholder groups.

GAO reported that many VA users said they were not adequately trained and that user-satisfaction goals were not established. The lesson is to define readiness and verify capability rather than count training attendance alone.

# Part IV. Responsible AI in project work

## AI use-case record

1. Purpose and decision supported.
2. Users and affected stakeholders.
3. Model, provider, data, and tools.
4. Data rights, privacy, classification, and retention.
5. Accuracy, reliability, bias, and limitations.
6. Security and misuse risk.
7. Human oversight and decision authority.
8. Testing and validation.
9. Monitoring, drift, and incident response.
10. Rollback, disablement, and exit.
11. Cost and business value.
12. Evidence and approval.

**NARRATOR**

AI can support planning, summarization, risk identification, forecasting, communication, and knowledge retrieval. It should not be treated as independent authority.

Project leaders should distinguish:

1. AI-generated suggestion.
2. Verified evidence.
3. Human analysis.
4. Authorized decision.
5. Outcome verification.

## Original AI scenario

A project team uses an AI tool to summarize stakeholder interviews. The tool omits concerns expressed by a minority group and creates two statements not found in the transcripts.

**Strongest action:** Stop relying on the summary, preserve the source interviews, validate output, assess data and bias controls, correct the stakeholder analysis, disclose the limitation, and redesign the review process before continued use.

# Part V. Sustainability and life-cycle value

Sustainability analysis should examine material environmental, social, operational, and economic effects across the life cycle.

A sustainability claim should identify:

1. Scope and boundary.
2. Baseline.
3. Measure and method.
4. Data source.
5. Time period.
6. Assumptions.
7. Trade-offs and displaced impact.
8. Owner.
9. Confidence and limitations.
10. Verification.

Do not claim a quantified benefit because a technology is described as green, digital, cloud-based, automated, or AI-enabled.

# Part VI. Documented public case: improvement, user concerns, and deployment readiness

GAO reported that VA paused electronic-health-record deployments because of user concerns and worked on incremental improvements during the pause. GAO also reported the need for updated cost and schedule evidence and significant stakeholder coordination for complex work.

**Lessons:**

1. User feedback can require a change in deployment strategy.
2. Incremental activity needs measurable readiness criteria.
3. Cost, schedule, risk, adoption, and technical evidence must remain integrated.
4. Improvement does not automatically prove readiness.
5. Stakeholder coordination remains part of delivery, not a communication afterthought.

**Limitations:** Federal healthcare context with many interacting causes. Reverify time-sensitive facts before mastering.

# Part VII. Integrated business capstone: Helios Enterprise Transformation

## Scenario

Helios is transforming customer operations across 14 countries.

The program includes:

1. A cloud platform and mobile application.
2. Data migration from eight legacy systems.
3. An AI assistant for customer support.
4. A global supplier and three regional subcontractors.
5. New privacy, accessibility, and security controls.
6. Workforce role changes and training.
7. A 42-million-dollar budget.
8. A public launch commitment in nine months.

New evidence arrives:

1. Adoption in the pilot is 31 percent.
2. The AI assistant creates unsupported answers in 4 percent of sampled complex cases.
3. One region has a new data-transfer restriction.
4. The critical supplier is six weeks late.
5. Accessibility testing finds a launch-blocking defect.
6. The cost forecast increases by 5.5 million dollars.
7. A sustainability claim lacks a verified baseline.
8. The sponsor wants to preserve the public launch date.

## Learner deliverables

1. Executive situation brief.
2. Stakeholder and authority map.
3. Integrated risk and issue assessment.
4. Delivery-approach and recovery recommendation.
5. AI governance and human-oversight plan.
6. Compliance and accessibility decision record.
7. Updated schedule and financial forecast.
8. Adoption and organizational-change plan.
9. Benefit and sustainability measurement plan.
10. Board-ready recommendation with limitations and verification.

## Capstone decision lens

1. Environment: predictive, adaptive, hybrid, regulated, operational.
2. Evidence: facts, sources, quality, freshness, confidence.
3. Authority: sponsor, product, risk, legal, accessibility, security, finance, board.
4. Sequence: contain, clarify, analyze, decide, implement, verify.
5. Value and protection: outcomes, people, obligations, risk, cost, schedule.
6. Verification: measurable conditions for release and recovery.

## Strong response pattern

A strong learner response does not protect the original date at all costs or stop everything without analysis. It:

1. Identifies launch blockers and authority.
2. Contains AI and data risk.
3. Corrects accessibility and regional compliance requirements.
4. Rebuilds the critical path and forecast.
5. Analyzes phased release, scope, supplier, and recovery options.
6. Establishes adoption and capability measures.
7. Removes unsupported sustainability claims.
8. Presents options and recommendation to governance.
9. Defines conditions, owners, residual risk, and verification.

# Part VIII. PMP exam-readiness plan

## Readiness evidence

1. Domain performance by People, Process, and Business Environment.
2. Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid scenario performance.
3. Question-format performance.
4. Timing by 60-question section.
5. Confidence calibration.
6. Recurring reasoning errors.
7. Formula and artifact fluency.
8. Ability to identify authority and next action.
9. Full simulation performance.
10. Fatigue and break strategy.

## Study cycle

1. Diagnose weak tasks.
2. Review authoritative content and course instruction.
3. Use retrieval practice.
4. Complete original scenarios.
5. Explain reasoning aloud.
6. Apply the concept to a business artifact.
7. Retest after spaced delay.
8. Measure improvement.

## Common final traps

1. Memorize terminology without scenario reasoning.
2. Choose the most forceful action before analysis.
3. Escalate too early or too late.
4. Let the project manager exceed authority.
5. Ignore stakeholder and business impact.
6. Confuse agile with no governance.
7. Hide uncertainty or risk.
8. Select an answer that bypasses process when no emergency exists.
9. Use AI or exam dumps to undermine assessment integrity.
10. Treat practice score as a guarantee.

## Knowledge checks

1. A repeated lesson has no owner or action. **Assign improvement and verify implementation.**
2. Adoption is low despite completed training. **Analyze workflow, capability, incentives, support, and stakeholder evidence.**
3. An external regulation changes. **Verify applicability, analyze impact, engage authority, and govern change.**
4. AI output conflicts with source evidence. **Use the source, stop unsafe reliance, investigate, correct, and govern continued use.**
5. Sustainability benefit lacks a baseline. **Do not make the quantified claim until evidence and limitations are established.**

## Closing

Projects operate inside changing organizations and environments. Improve the system, support adoption, monitor external change, govern AI, measure sustainability honestly, and connect every decision to value, protection, authority, and evidence.

The instructional curriculum is complete only when the learner can integrate all three PMP domains in one business decision.

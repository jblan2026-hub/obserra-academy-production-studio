## Scene 1. Cold open: the feature delivered before the control, 00:09 to 02:25

**VISUAL**

A digital product team has capacity for one major item before launch.

Option A adds a customer-facing AI assistant expected to increase conversion.

Option B completes identity controls required to prevent unauthorized data exposure.

Option C automates a manual support process expected to reduce operating cost.

Option D fixes a known accessibility barrier affecting a group of customers.

The sponsor asks:

> Which item creates the most value?

**NARRATOR**

The answer is not always the item with the largest revenue estimate.

Value includes benefits, cost, risk, timing, stakeholder impact, obligations, strategic purpose, uncertainty, and future options. Some work protects value rather than creating visible features.

## Scene 3. Value is multi-dimensional, 03:10 to 06:45

**VISUAL**

A value model appears with nine dimensions.

1. Strategic alignment.
2. Customer or beneficiary outcome.
3. Financial effect.
4. Risk reduction or opportunity creation.
5. Safety, security, privacy, compliance, and ethical obligations.
6. Time sensitivity and opportunity window.
7. Operational capability and resilience.
8. Stakeholder impact and trust.
9. Learning and future options.

**NARRATOR**

The weight of each dimension depends on the organization and project.

A nonprofit may prioritize mission impact. A regulated healthcare organization may prioritize patient safety and compliance. A commercial product may prioritize customer outcome, revenue, retention, and strategic position. A cybersecurity project may create value by reducing exposure and improving resilience.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Value cannot be reduced to one universal formula.

## Scene 4. Prioritization criteria and governance, 06:45 to 10:45

**VISUAL**

A transparent prioritization record appears.

1. Item or outcome.
2. Stakeholder and benefit.
3. Estimated value.
4. Cost and effort.
5. Risk reduction or risk introduced.
6. Time criticality.
7. Dependency and sequencing.
8. Compliance or contractual obligation.
9. Learning value.
10. Confidence and assumptions.
11. Decision authority.
12. Review date.

**NARRATOR**

Prioritization should be transparent enough that stakeholders understand why one item moves ahead of another.

Common techniques include:

1. Cost of delay.
2. Weighted scoring.
3. Value versus effort.
4. Risk-adjusted value.
5. Must, should, could, and will not prioritization.
6. Opportunity scoring.
7. Economic sequencing.
8. Dependency and compliance gating.

The technique is less important than the quality of inputs, assumptions, decision authority, and follow-through.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A security control may have limited direct revenue but high mandatory or risk-protection value. A feature with high estimated revenue may depend on unresolved data rights and model risk. A transparent model prevents one number from dominating the decision.

## Scene 5. Cost of delay and simple economic reasoning, 10:45 to 14:15

**VISUAL**

Three items compete.

### Item A

Expected weekly benefit: 100,000 dollars.  
Estimated duration: 4 weeks.

### Item B

Expected weekly benefit or loss avoided: 60,000 dollars.  
Estimated duration: 1 week.

### Item C

Expected weekly benefit: 140,000 dollars.  
Estimated duration: 8 weeks.

A simplified cost-of-delay-divided-by-duration calculation appears.

A: 100,000 divided by 4 = 25,000.  
B: 60,000 divided by 1 = 60,000.  
C: 140,000 divided by 8 = 17,500.

**NARRATOR**

In this simplified model, Item B has the highest ratio and may be sequenced first.

But the calculation is not sufficient by itself.

The project should still consider:

1. Whether the value estimate is credible.
2. Whether items are independent.
3. Whether risk or obligation creates a required sequence.
4. Whether duration includes integration and acceptance.
5. Whether the value begins immediately after completion.
6. Whether the item creates negative stakeholder or technical consequences.

**LIMITATION CARD**

A prioritization ratio is a decision aid, not an automatic decision.

## Scene 6. Incremental value and minimum viable delivery, 14:15 to 17:30

**VISUAL**

A large product release decomposes into four useful increments.

1. Limited pilot.
2. Core workflow.
3. Broader integration.
4. Advanced automation.

**NARRATOR**

Incremental delivery can reduce time to learning and value. It allows the project to test assumptions before committing all investment.

A minimum viable product or capability should be the smallest release that can test the critical value and risk assumptions with real stakeholders. It is not the lowest-quality product the team can release.

A viable increment should identify:

1. Target stakeholder and outcome.
2. Hypothesis or value assumption.
3. Minimum functional and quality conditions.
4. Security, safety, compliance, and ethical guardrails.
5. Measurement plan.
6. Support and rollback.
7. Decision after evidence.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Deliver the smallest responsible increment that creates valid learning or value.

## Scene 7. Benefits realization and ownership, 17:30 to 21:05

**VISUAL**

A benefit profile appears.

1. Benefit statement.
2. Beneficiary.
3. Baseline.
4. Target and timing.
5. Measure and data source.
6. Assumptions and dependencies.
7. Project contribution.
8. Operational contribution.
9. Benefit owner.
10. Review cadence and corrective action.

**NARRATOR**

Benefits often occur after delivery and depend on operational adoption, policy change, process redesign, behavior, market conditions, or retirement of legacy capability.

The project manager should clarify which benefits the project can enable and which require continued operational ownership.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A new procurement platform does not create savings merely by going live. Savings may require supplier adoption, process compliance, contract renegotiation, data quality, workforce behavior, and retirement of legacy systems.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Do not assign the project credit or blame for outcomes beyond its influence without analyzing dependencies.

## Scene 8. Guardrails and negative value, 21:05 to 23:45

**VISUAL**

A value dashboard includes guardrails.

1. Safety incidents.
2. Security exposure.
3. Privacy impact.
4. Accessibility barriers.
5. Compliance exceptions.
6. Customer harm.
7. Workforce impact.
8. Technical debt.
9. Environmental impact.
10. Concentration and resilience risk.

**NARRATOR**

A project can produce a benefit and still create unacceptable harm or residual risk.

Guardrails define conditions that the project should not violate while pursuing value.

The project manager should not trade away controlling obligations through a prioritization score. Material ethical, legal, safety, and risk decisions belong to authorized owners and governance.

## Scene 9. Documented public case: incremental improvement after deployment pause, 23:45 to 27:20

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
Electronic Health Records: VA Making Incremental Improvements in New System but Needs Updated Cost Estimate and Schedule  
GAO 25 106874  
Published 12 March 2025  
Source ID: GAO VA EHR INCREMENTAL IMPROVEMENT 2025

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that the Department of Veterans Affairs paused deployments of its modernized electronic health record after user concerns. During the pause, VA worked on incremental configuration and system improvements.

The case illustrates a value-delivery challenge.

Incremental improvement can reduce risk and address user needs, but the project still requires evidence that changes create sufficient readiness and value. GAO also reported the need for an updated life-cycle cost estimate and integrated schedule reflecting changes and delays.

**NARRATOR**

Activity is not evidence of value. The project should define which outcome each increment is intended to improve, how it will be measured, and what decision follows.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

1. Federal healthcare modernization context.
2. The program has many technical, clinical, contractual, and organizational factors.
3. Incremental changes do not prove that the system is ready or that all stakeholder concerns are resolved.
4. Time-sensitive facts must be reverified before mastering.

## Scene 10. Original enterprise scenario: Sentinel AI Assistant, 27:20 to 31:15

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Sentinel is preparing an AI assistant for customer support.

The team can complete one item before pilot:

A. Add multilingual response generation expected to increase adoption.  
B. Complete access controls and data-loss prevention for sensitive uploads.  
C. Automate case summaries expected to reduce handling time.  
D. Improve accessibility for keyboard and screen-reader users.

Additional facts:

1. Sensitive-data controls are required by policy before external pilot.
2. Accessibility defects prevent a stakeholder group from completing the workflow.
3. Multilingual demand is high but the evaluation data is incomplete.
4. Case summaries have measurable internal value and low external risk.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do?

**OPTIONS**

A. Prioritize only the highest revenue estimate.  
B. Ask engineering to choose the easiest item.  
C. Apply transparent value, obligation, risk, stakeholder impact, dependency, and learning criteria with the authorized product and governance owners.  
D. Deliver all items without changing time or resources.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The security control may be a release prerequisite. The accessibility issue represents stakeholder harm and readiness. The internal summary may create low-risk value. The multilingual feature may require further evidence.

**FOLLOW UP**

Governance requires B and D before external pilot. The team delivers C to internal users in parallel and runs a controlled evaluation for A. The roadmap, measures, risks, and stakeholder communications are updated.

## Scene 11. Stop, pivot, continue, or scale, 31:15 to 33:15

**VISUAL**

An evidence review leads to four decisions.

### Continue

Evidence supports the hypothesis and current investment.

### Scale

Value, quality, controls, support, and capacity justify broader delivery.

### Pivot

Evidence supports a different solution or stakeholder outcome.

### Stop

Expected value no longer justifies cost, risk, opportunity, or strategic fit.

**NARRATOR**

Stopping low-value work is not failure when it protects resources and prevents harm. Continuing because of sunk cost can destroy value.

The decision should consider evidence, remaining cost, opportunity cost, risk, stakeholder impact, contractual obligations, and authority.

## Scene 12. Common PMP value traps, 33:15 to 35:00

### Trap 1. Prioritize the highest revenue estimate

Consider confidence, risk, time, dependency, obligations, and impact.

### Trap 2. Treat mandatory controls as non-value work

Controls can protect value, trust, safety, and authorization to operate.

### Trap 3. Equate completion with benefit

Measure outcomes after transition.

### Trap 4. Build a minimum product by removing quality

Minimum viable still requires responsible guardrails and fitness for purpose.

### Trap 5. Continue because money has already been spent

Use future value, cost, risk, and alternatives, not sunk cost alone.

### Trap 6. Own benefits only inside the project team

Confirm operational benefit ownership and review after transition.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A feature has high estimated revenue but unresolved data-rights and security risks. What should the project manager do?

A. Prioritize it automatically.  
B. Analyze value, confidence, risk, obligations, impact, and decision authority before prioritization.  
C. Hide the risks.  
D. Reject all innovation.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

What is the purpose of a minimum viable product?

A. Release the lowest-quality product.  
B. Deliver the smallest responsible increment that tests critical value and risk assumptions.  
C. Avoid acceptance criteria.  
D. Eliminate stakeholder involvement.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

Who should own a benefit expected two years after project closure?

A. No one after closure.  
B. A named operational or business owner with measures and review cadence, supported by project transition.  
C. The scheduler.  
D. The supplier automatically.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

Evidence shows that the expected value no longer justifies remaining cost and risk. What should the project manager do?

A. Continue because of sunk cost.  
B. Present evidence and options to the authorized governance owner for a stop, pivot, or continuation decision.  
C. Hide the evidence.  
D. Spend the remaining budget quickly.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 35:00 to 36:10

**NARRATOR**

Value-based delivery prioritizes outcomes, risk, obligations, timing, stakeholder impact, learning, and strategic purpose, not features alone.

Deliver responsible increments when possible. Measure benefits beyond completion. Assign operational ownership. Use guardrails. Stop, pivot, continue, or scale based on current evidence and authorized decisions.

On the PMP exam, identify what creates and protects value, who owns the decision, what evidence is available, and how the outcome will be verified.

**ON SCREEN**

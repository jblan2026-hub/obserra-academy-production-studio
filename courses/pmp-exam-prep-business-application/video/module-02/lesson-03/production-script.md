## Scene 3. Five concepts that must not be confused, 03:05 to 06:45

**VISUAL**

Five terms appear as connected but distinct elements.

### Role

A defined position in the project system, such as sponsor, project manager, product owner, technical lead, risk owner, control owner, approver, or operational owner.

### Responsibility

The work or obligation a person or group is expected to perform.

### Accountability

The obligation to answer for a decision, outcome, or control within defined authority.

### Authority

The legitimate power to decide, approve, commit resources, accept risk, change scope, represent the organization, or direct action.

### Ownership

A practical assignment of ongoing responsibility for an artifact, risk, benefit, product, process, decision, or outcome.

**NARRATOR**

A person can be responsible for analysis without being authorized to approve the decision. A project manager can be accountable for the integrity of the project process without being authorized to accept enterprise risk. A sponsor can own the business case while an operational leader owns benefit realization after transition.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Expertise does not automatically create authority. Assignment does not automatically create accountability. Urgency does not automatically change either one.

## Scene 4. The decision rights model, 06:45 to 10:40

**VISUAL**

A decision record appears with eight fields.

1. Decision question.
2. Accountable decision owner.
3. Required participants and advisors.
4. Evidence required.
5. Constraints and policies.
6. Approval or escalation threshold.
7. Effective date and implementation owner.
8. Verification and review date.

**NARRATOR**

A task matrix is not enough for high consequence work. The project also needs a decision rights model.

A useful model clarifies:

1. Who frames the question.
2. Who prepares analysis.
3. Who provides specialist advice.
4. Who decides.
5. Who approves when approval is distinct from the decision.
6. Who implements.
7. Who independently verifies.
8. Who owns the outcome after transition.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A cybersecurity program must decide whether to delay a product release because of a high severity vulnerability.

Engineering owns remediation analysis. Security evaluates exploitability and exposure. Product assesses customer and schedule impact. Legal considers contractual and disclosure obligations. The project manager integrates the decision process. The risk acceptance authority makes the decision according to policy. Operations implements and verifies the approved action.

No one role should be assumed to own every part.

## Scene 5. Responsibility matrices and their limitations, 10:40 to 13:15

**VISUAL**

A responsibility matrix forms, then highlights ambiguity.

**NARRATOR**

Responsibility matrices can clarify participation. One familiar pattern identifies who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed. Organizations may use other models or their own terminology.

The tool is useful only when the definitions match organizational authority.

Common failures include:

1. Multiple accountable roles for one decision without a tie breaking mechanism.
2. A named accountable person who lacks formal authority.
3. Everyone listed as consulted, creating delay and diluted ownership.
4. Critical specialists omitted.
5. The matrix describes routine work but not exceptions or emergencies.
6. The matrix is created once and never updated.
7. Benefit and operational ownership disappear at transition.

**NARRATOR**

The exam may present a role conflict without naming a matrix. Look for unclear ownership, missing authority, overlapping decisions, or a responsibility gap.

## Scene 6. Empowerment with governance, 13:15 to 16:15

**VISUAL**

An empowered team operates inside a visible boundary. The boundary contains purpose, delegated decisions, risk thresholds, compliance constraints, resources, and escalation rules.

**NARRATOR**

Empowerment means giving people meaningful authority, information, resources, and support to make decisions within defined boundaries.

Empowerment is not the absence of governance. Strong governance makes safe empowerment possible because the team understands what it may decide and when it must escalate.

An empowerment agreement should identify:

1. Desired outcome.
2. Decisions the team may make.
3. Decisions reserved to another authority.
4. Risk and cost thresholds.
5. Required evidence.
6. Security, safety, regulatory, ethical, and contractual constraints.
7. Reporting cadence.
8. Escalation triggers.
9. Verification method.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Autonomy grows when purpose, evidence, authority, and boundaries are clear.

## Scene 7. Guidance based application: integrated schedule and cost ownership, 16:15 to 19:15

**VISUAL**

An integrated program schedule and cost model appear side by side. Activities, dependencies, risk reserves, and decision milestones connect across workstreams.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
GAO Schedule Assessment Guide, GAO 16 89G  
GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide, GAO 20 195G

**NARRATOR**

GAO guidance emphasizes integrated schedules and reliable cost estimates as decision tools. An integrated schedule helps show whether milestones are realistic and how proposed changes affect the overall program. Reliable cost estimates support budgeting, oversight, and performance evaluation.

These artifacts require named ownership.

The schedule owner may maintain the integrated model, but workstream leads own the quality and currency of their inputs. Risk owners provide response and uncertainty information. Finance and cost specialists maintain estimating assumptions. Governance authorities approve material baseline changes. The project manager ensures integration and decision use.

**NARRATOR**

If one person is labeled “responsible for the schedule” while other teams can change dates, dependencies, resources, or scope without updating it, the schedule will not remain decision useful.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

Government guidance does not prescribe one universal organizational chart. Each organization must define roles consistent with its authority, contracts, policies, and operating model.

## Scene 8. Accountability through transition, 19:15 to 21:45

**VISUAL**

A project moves from delivery to operations. Several ownership tokens cross the transition boundary:

1. Product ownership.
2. Service ownership.
3. Control ownership.
4. Risk ownership.
5. Benefit ownership.
6. Data ownership.
7. Vendor ownership.
8. Evidence and records ownership.

**NARRATOR**

Projects often lose accountability at transition. The project team completes delivery, but operational ownership is incomplete or assumed.

A strong transition confirms:

1. Who accepts the product or capability.
2. Who operates and supports it.
3. Who owns residual risk.
4. Who monitors controls and performance.
5. Who measures benefits.
6. Who manages vendors and renewals.
7. Who retains evidence and records.
8. Who has authority to change or retire the capability.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A new identity governance platform is deployed. The project closes, but no operational owner is assigned for access review exceptions. Exceptions accumulate and the intended risk reduction weakens.

The output was delivered. Accountability for the operating control was not.

## Scene 9. Ethical accountability and escalation, 21:45 to 24:05

**VISUAL**

A project manager is pressured to sign an approval outside their authority. The signature field glows red while the evidence record remains incomplete.

**NARRATOR**

Ethical leadership requires accurate representation of authority and evidence.

Responsibility means acknowledging decisions and consequences. Respect means recognizing specialist roles and affected stakeholders. Fairness means applying consistent criteria and avoiding hidden privilege. Honesty means not presenting analysis as approval, not claiming certainty that does not exist, and not accepting authority one does not possess.

If a leader is asked to approve outside their authority, the strongest response is to document the evidence, clarify the boundary, identify the authorized owner, and escalate through the approved process.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Do not solve an accountability gap by inventing authority.

## Scene 10. Original enterprise scenario: Helios Global Release, 24:05 to 28:10

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Helios is preparing a global customer platform release.

1. Engineering confirms technical readiness.
2. Security reports a residual identity risk.
3. Legal reports that one regional contract requires additional notice.
4. Product owns the release roadmap.
5. The steering committee meets in ten days.
6. The sponsor tells the project manager to “own the release decision” to avoid delay.
7. The project charter does not give the project manager risk acceptance or contractual waiver authority.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Approve the release because the sponsor delegated it verbally.  
B. Ask engineering to approve the release because it completed the work.  
C. Clarify the decision rights, assemble the required evidence, identify the authorized risk and contractual decision owners, and expedite the governance decision.  
D. Delay the release indefinitely without involving governance.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The project manager leads the decision process without assuming authority that is not established.

A relies on an informal instruction that may not lawfully or organizationally transfer authority. B confuses technical responsibility with enterprise decision authority. D avoids the decision rather than governing it.

**FOLLOW UP**

The organization confirms that the product executive may approve the release only if the risk owner accepts the identity exposure and legal confirms the notice plan.

The project manager updates the decision record, coordinates the evidence, confirms each approval, communicates the conditions, and assigns implementation and verification owners.

## Scene 11. Common PMP role and accountability traps, 28:10 to 30:20

### Trap 1. The project manager decides everything

The project manager integrates and leads the project system but may not own every technical, legal, financial, operational, or risk decision.

### Trap 2. The sponsor owns every issue

The sponsor provides authority and strategic support but should not become the default owner for routine team decisions.

### Trap 3. Empowerment means no escalation

Empowered teams still escalate decisions beyond delegated authority or risk thresholds.

### Trap 4. The person doing the work is accountable for the outcome

Responsibility for execution and accountability for a decision may be assigned to different roles.

### Trap 5. A detailed matrix proves role clarity

Role clarity must work in realistic scenarios, exceptions, transitions, and emergencies.

### Trap 6. Verbal delegation always transfers authority

Authority transfer must be consistent with law, policy, contracts, governance, and documented organizational rules.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A technical lead completes a risk analysis but does not have authority to accept the risk. What should the project manager do?

A. Treat the analysis as approval.  
B. Route the analysis to the authorized risk owner and preserve the decision record.  
C. Ask the technical lead to sign on behalf of the sponsor.  
D. Remove the risk from the register.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which condition best supports team empowerment?

A. The team receives broad goals but no decision boundaries.  
B. The team has purpose, information, delegated authority, resources, constraints, escalation thresholds, and verification.  
C. The sponsor stops attending governance meetings.  
D. The project manager stops monitoring outcomes.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A role matrix lists two accountable executives for one approval and does not define how disagreement is resolved. What is the primary issue?

A. There are too few consulted stakeholders.  
B. The decision accountability and tie breaking authority are unclear.  
C. The team needs a longer schedule.  
D. The project manager should choose the preferred executive.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

A project has completed delivery, but no one is assigned to own benefit measurement after transition. What should the project manager do?

A. Close the project because delivery is complete.  
B. Confirm the accountable benefit owner and transition the measure, evidence, and review cadence before closure.  
C. Assign benefit ownership to the project scheduler.  
D. Remove benefits from the business case.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 30:20 to 31:30

**NARRATOR**

Projects need more than assigned tasks. They need clear roles, accountable decisions, legitimate authority, empowered execution, and verified ownership through transition.

Separate who performs the work from who decides, approves, accepts risk, verifies, and owns the outcome.

On the PMP exam, when accountability is unclear, do not guess or assume authority. Clarify the decision model, engage the correct roles, use governance, document the result, and verify implementation.

**ON SCREEN**

> Name the role. Clarify the authority. Empower the work. Preserve accountability.

## Scene 1. Cold open: passed acceptance, failed in operation, 00:09 to 02:40

**VISUAL**

A new enterprise reporting platform passes its final project acceptance test.

Thirty days later:

1. Some calculations use inconsistent data definitions.
2. Screen-reader users cannot complete critical workflows.
3. Retention controls do not match one regional requirement.
4. Peak-load performance is below the operating target.
5. Support teams lack troubleshooting evidence.
6. The carbon-impact claim in the business case has not been measured.
7. The project team says every contracted test passed.

**NARRATOR**

Acceptance is not the same as fitness for purpose. Test completion is not the same as quality. A compliance checklist is not proof that obligations are satisfied in operation.

Quality management integrates requirements, design, process, evidence, prevention, control, verification, acceptance, and continuous improvement across the project life cycle.

## Scene 3. What quality means, 03:25 to 06:55

**VISUAL**

A quality model appears with four dimensions.

### Conformance

Does the output meet approved requirements, specifications, criteria, and obligations?

### Fitness for use

Can intended users and operators use the result safely, reliably, accessibly, and effectively in the intended environment?

### Outcome quality

Does the result enable the intended operational and stakeholder outcomes?

### Process quality

Does the project use capable, controlled, repeatable, and evidence-producing processes appropriate to risk?

**NARRATOR**

Quality is not luxury, decoration, or maximum performance. It is the degree to which the result and the process satisfy relevant requirements and intended use.

A technically excellent feature can be low quality if it is inaccessible, insecure, unreliable, unsupported, legally unusable, or disconnected from the intended outcome.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Quality is designed, produced, verified, and sustained. It cannot be added reliably at the final gate.

The lesson for project leaders is to tailor quality practices without weakening the requirements that control fitness, safety, compliance, or stakeholder impact.

## Scene 5. Verification, validation, control, assurance, audit, and acceptance, 10:35 to 14:45

**VISUAL**

Six activities appear as distinct controls.

### Verification

Did the work product meet specified requirements or design criteria?

### Validation

Does the final or integrated result meet intended use and stakeholder needs in the relevant operating context?

### Quality control

Operational techniques used to inspect, measure, test, and detect nonconformance.

### Quality assurance

Confidence-building activities that evaluate whether processes and controls are capable and followed.

### Audit or independent assessment

A structured review performed under defined authority and criteria, often by a role independent of the work being assessed.

### Acceptance

A formal decision by an authorized stakeholder that defined conditions have been met or that specified exceptions are approved.

**NARRATOR**

These controls may overlap, but they answer different questions.

A product can pass verification and fail validation. A team can follow its process consistently while the process itself is inadequate. An audit can identify evidence gaps without making the product-acceptance decision.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Do not treat one test, signature, certificate, or audit as universal proof of quality or compliance.

## Scene 6. Cost of quality, 14:45 to 18:25

**VISUAL**

The cost of quality divides into conformance and nonconformance.

### Prevention cost

Training, design reviews, secure development, supplier qualification, process improvement, and mistake-proofing.

### Appraisal cost

Testing, inspection, audit, validation, and independent review.

### Internal failure cost

Rework, scrap, retesting, defect correction, delay, and failed builds before release.

### External failure cost

Customer impact, recalls, incidents, warranty, regulatory action, reputational damage, and emergency support after release.

**WORKED EXAMPLE**

A project spends:

Prevention: 120,000 dollars.  
Appraisal: 80,000 dollars.  
Internal failure: 150,000 dollars.  
External failure: 400,000 dollars.

Total conformance cost:

> 120,000 + 80,000 = 200,000 dollars.

Total nonconformance cost:

> 150,000 + 400,000 = 550,000 dollars.

Total quality-related cost:

> 200,000 + 550,000 = 750,000 dollars.

**NARRATOR**

The calculation does not prove that any prevention investment will automatically reduce failure by a known amount. It makes the current distribution visible and supports analysis.

## Scene 7. Compliance by design and traceability, 18:25 to 22:20

**VISUAL**

An obligation moves through a traceability chain.

Source obligation.  
Applicability decision.  
Requirement.  
Control.  
Owner.  
Implementation.  
Test.  
Evidence.  
Exception.  
Approval.  
Monitoring.

**NARRATOR**

Compliance should begin with determining what actually applies.

Sources may include:

1. Law and regulation.
2. Contract.
3. License or permit.
4. Organizational policy.
5. Professional or industry standard.
6. Safety or security requirement.
7. Customer commitment.
8. Voluntary framework adopted by the organization.

The project manager should engage qualified authorities to translate applicable obligations into traceable project requirements.

**ON SCREEN DISTINCTION**

> A framework can guide. A contract can bind. A law or regulation can compel. An internal policy can govern organizational behavior. They are not interchangeable.

**NARRATOR**

Exceptions should identify authority, rationale, duration, compensating controls, residual risk, review, and evidence. The project manager should not declare compliance based on self-assessment alone when independent validation is required.

## Scene 8. Sustainability as a project decision dimension, 22:20 to 25:05

**VISUAL**

A sustainability model appears with environmental, social, operational, and economic dimensions.

1. Energy and emissions.
2. Materials, waste, and circularity.
3. Water and land impact.
4. Workforce and community impact.
5. Accessibility and inclusion.
6. Supply-chain practices.
7. Resilience and life-cycle durability.
8. Total life-cycle cost and benefit.

**NARRATOR**

The July 2026 PMP exam increases the emphasis on sustainability and business impact.

Sustainability should not be reduced to a marketing claim. The project should define the material impact, baseline, method, source, owner, target, uncertainty, and decision use.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A cloud migration claims lower environmental impact because physical servers will be retired. The project should examine workload growth, data-center region, energy source, utilization, equipment retirement, network use, and measurement method before claiming a quantified benefit.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Do not claim a sustainability benefit that the project cannot measure or reasonably support.

GAO reported data-quality problems in selected transferred information in the Department of Veterans Affairs' electronic health record modernization. GAO also reported that system reports were made available without consulting all users, creating uncertainty about whether reports met user needs.

**NARRATOR**

The project-quality lesson is integrated:

1. Data conformance matters.
2. Reports must be validated against user and decision needs.
3. Training activity should be verified through capability and performance.
4. Satisfaction needs measurable goals when it is a project objective.
5. Quality must continue after technical delivery.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

1. Federal healthcare modernization context.
2. Patient, clinical, technical, contractual, and organizational factors interact.
3. The reports do not establish one single cause.
4. The findings should be reverified before final production.

## Scene 10. Original enterprise scenario: Nova Sustainable Platform Release, 29:15 to 33:50

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Nova is releasing a global procurement platform.

1. Functional acceptance tests pass.
2. One region's retention control is not implemented.
3. Keyboard-only users cannot complete supplier onboarding.
4. Peak-load performance is below target.
5. A supplier security report is expired.
6. The business case claims a 30 percent energy reduction without a verified baseline.
7. The sponsor wants to launch and treat all findings as post-release improvements.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Launch because functional tests passed.  
B. Cancel the project immediately.  
C. Classify each finding, verify applicable requirements and evidence, engage the authorized quality, legal, accessibility, security, operational, and sponsor roles, and route the release and exception decision through governance.  
D. Hide the findings from the customer.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The project manager should not independently waive requirements or treat all findings as equivalent.

**FOLLOW UP**

Governance determines that the retention and accessibility issues block launch, performance requires a controlled limitation, supplier evidence must be refreshed, and the sustainability claim must be removed until measured. The team corrects the blocking findings, updates evidence, retests, and obtains authorized acceptance.

## Scene 11. Nonconformance and corrective action, 33:50 to 35:45

**VISUAL**

A nonconformance moves through a controlled path.

1. Detect and preserve evidence.
2. Classify severity and applicability.
3. Contain immediate impact.
4. Assign owner.
5. Analyze cause.
6. Correct the specific issue.
7. Implement corrective action to reduce recurrence.
8. Verify effectiveness.
9. Update risk, lessons, and controls.
10. Close under defined authority.

**NARRATOR**

Correction fixes the observed problem. Corrective action addresses cause and recurrence. Preventive improvement addresses vulnerabilities before failure.

The project manager should distinguish these actions in reporting and closure.

## Scene 12. Common PMP quality and compliance traps, 35:45 to 37:35

### Trap 1. Inspect quality only at the end

Build quality and evidence into planning and execution.

### Trap 2. Treat acceptance as proof of fitness for every use

Validate intended use and operational context.

### Trap 3. Treat a framework as a legal requirement

Determine applicability and authority.

### Trap 4. Add more testing without cause analysis

Improve the process and control system where needed.

### Trap 5. Waive a compliance issue without authority

Use the approved exception and risk-acceptance process.

### Trap 6. Make an unsupported sustainability claim

Define and verify the baseline, method, data, and limitations.

### Trap 7. Count training completion as capability

Verify performance in the intended environment.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A deliverable meets specification but fails in the intended operating environment. Which concept is most directly at issue?

A. Fitness for use and validation.  
B. Budget authority only.  
C. Procurement competition.  
D. Meeting attendance.

**Correct response:** A.

### Question 2

Prevention cost is 120,000 dollars and appraisal cost is 80,000 dollars. What is total conformance cost?

A. 40,000 dollars.  
B. 200,000 dollars.  
C. 550,000 dollars.  
D. 750,000 dollars.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A legal requirement is not implemented before launch. What should the project manager do?

A. Waive it personally.  
B. Verify applicability, preserve evidence, and engage the authorized legal and governance process before release.  
C. Hide the issue.  
D. Rename it as technical debt.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

A sustainability benefit has no verified baseline. What should the project manager do?

A. Publish the claim.  
B. Define the measure and source, establish or reconstruct an approved baseline if credible, state limitations, and avoid unsupported claims.  
C. Estimate a number from memory.  
D. Remove all sustainability analysis.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 37:35 to 38:45

**NARRATOR**

Quality connects requirements, intended use, process capability, evidence, acceptance, and operational outcome. Compliance connects applicable obligations to controls, owners, tests, exceptions, and monitoring. Sustainability connects life-cycle impact to measurable decisions.

On the PMP exam, do not wait for final inspection or waive obligations informally. Determine what applies, build it into the work, verify and validate, preserve evidence, govern exceptions, correct causes, and verify effectiveness.

**ON SCREEN**

> Design quality. Trace obligations. Verify evidence. Govern exceptions. Sustain the outcome.

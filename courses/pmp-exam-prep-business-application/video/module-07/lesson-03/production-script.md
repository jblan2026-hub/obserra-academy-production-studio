## Cold open: the approved change that never reached the project

A steering committee approves a new regional requirement. The decision is recorded in meeting notes but not reflected in the backlog, schedule, cost forecast, supplier statement of work, test plan, training, or customer communication. Three months later the project delivers the old design.

**NARRATOR**

A change is not controlled because it was discussed or approved. Change control connects request, analysis, authority, implementation, verification, and the authoritative project record.

## Change categories

1. Scope or requirement.
2. Product priority or backlog.
3. Schedule or milestone.
4. Cost, funding, or reserve.
5. Resource or organization.
6. Design, architecture, or configuration.
7. Quality, security, safety, privacy, or compliance.
8. Supplier or contract.
9. Operational transition.
10. Benefit, outcome, or strategy.
11. Corrective or preventive action.
12. Emergency containment.

The category helps identify analysis, authority, and records. A single change may belong to several categories.

## Clarification, defect, and change

A clarification explains an existing approved requirement without changing its meaning or commitment.

A defect is nonconformance with an approved requirement or criterion. Correcting it may still affect schedule, cost, risk, or contract and may require governed action.

A change modifies an approved requirement, plan, baseline, backlog priority, design, contract, control, or commitment.

**NARRATOR**

Do not classify every request as scope creep or every defect correction as cost-free. Analyze the actual effect.

## Integrated change record

1. Identifier and requester.
2. Requested outcome and rationale.
3. Current state.
4. Proposed change.
5. Urgency and effective date.
6. Value and stakeholder impact.
7. Scope, schedule, cost, resource, quality, risk, and benefit impact.
8. Security, privacy, safety, compliance, and accessibility impact.
9. Supplier, contract, and operational impact.
10. Options and recommendation.
11. Decision owner and authority.
12. Conditions, implementation, verification, and closure.

## Predictive change control

Predictive projects often use formal baselines and integrated change control. The project should:

1. Document the request.
2. Analyze integrated impact.
3. Obtain the authorized decision.
4. Update baselines and subsidiary plans.
5. Communicate the change.
6. Implement through configuration and work control.
7. Verify results.

Formal does not mean slow by design. Thresholds and delegated authority can allow efficient handling of low-impact changes.

## Adaptive change control

Adaptive teams expect backlog and solution details to evolve. Change is controlled through product goals, backlog authority, iteration or flow policies, architecture, acceptance, funding, release governance, and risk thresholds.

A product owner may reprioritize work within delegated authority. Material changes to product goal, funding, contract, compliance, enterprise risk, or external commitments may require broader governance.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Adaptive change is governed through explicit authority and product boundaries, not through the absence of control.

## Configuration control

Configuration management identifies and controls product versions, interfaces, code, data, documents, environments, models, infrastructure, and approved baselines.

A change should identify:

1. Which configuration items are affected.
2. Version and environment.
3. Dependencies.
4. Build and release evidence.
5. Rollback.
6. Authorized migration and effective date.
7. Records and traceability.

Without configuration control, an approved change may be implemented inconsistently across environments or customers.

## Emergency change

An emergency path may allow accelerated authorization and implementation when safety, security, service continuity, or material harm requires immediate action.

A controlled emergency change still needs:

1. Defined emergency criteria.
2. Authorized decision role.
3. Minimum evidence.
4. Containment and rollback.
5. Communication.
6. Post-implementation review.
7. Permanent correction and record update.

Urgency does not erase accountability.

## Guidance-based integration example

GAO schedule and cost guidance emphasizes maintaining controlled baselines and updating estimates and schedules when approved changes occur. The project-management lesson is that an approved change should produce one coherent current plan and forecast.

## Original scenario: Meridian Regulatory Change

A regulator publishes a new requirement during a hybrid platform project.

1. The requirement affects one data field and several retention processes.
2. The adaptive software team can change the feature quickly.
3. The supplier contract excludes the new interface.
4. The predictive validation plan is already approved.
5. The launch commitment is public.
6. Legal interpretation is not complete.

**Strongest action:** Engage qualified legal and compliance owners, document the change, analyze integrated product, validation, supplier, schedule, cost, risk, and stakeholder effects, identify authority, and update every affected artifact after decision.

The organization approves a phased change, modifies supplier scope, updates validation and release evidence, communicates revised regional timing, and records residual risk.

## Change implementation verification

After approval:

1. Confirm affected plans and artifacts are updated.
2. Confirm work and configuration changes are implemented.
3. Test and validate.
4. Confirm supplier and contract changes.
5. Communicate to stakeholders.
6. Update forecast and benefits.
7. Verify operational readiness.
8. Close the change record with evidence.

## Common exam traps

1. Implement before approval because value appears obvious.
2. Approve without integrated impact analysis.
3. Update the backlog but not contract, test, schedule, or communication.
4. Treat adaptive work as uncontrolled.
5. Use emergency change for convenience.
6. Assume an approved change is implemented.
7. Reject a regulatory or defect-related change as optional scope creep.

## Knowledge checks

1. A change is approved but plans are not updated. **Update all affected authoritative artifacts and verify implementation.**
2. A product owner reprioritizes within delegated authority. **Use the adaptive governance process; broader approval is not automatically required.**
3. A change affects a supplier contract. **Engage authorized procurement and contracting roles.**
4. An emergency action is needed. **Use the defined emergency path, contain risk, preserve records, and review afterward.**

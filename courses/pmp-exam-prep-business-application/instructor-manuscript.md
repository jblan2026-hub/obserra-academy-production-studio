# Part I — Course Intent

## 1. Course purpose

The course prepares learners for two related but distinct outcomes. The first is to reason through the current PMP examination using the July 2026 Examination Content Outline. The second is to apply the same project-management judgment inside real organizations. Learners should leave able to recognize the strongest next action in a scenario and able to produce project artifacts, decisions, and communications that improve delivery at work.

The course rejects shallow memorization. The July 2026 outline states that candidates are expected to apply project-management concepts and experience to on-the-job situations. The exam therefore rewards contextual judgment: identifying the domain, understanding the task, separating facts from assumptions, recognizing the project manager’s authority, engaging the correct people, following the appropriate process, protecting value, and preserving ethical accountability.

The course is built around 35 lessons totaling 2,100 instructional minutes. The instructional sequence follows the current domain distribution without turning the domain percentages into a mechanical question forecast. The design allocates substantial attention to People, Process, and Business Environment while integrating predictive, adaptive or agile, and hybrid approaches throughout.

## 2. Current exam facts

As of the source-verification date:

- People represents 33 percent of examination items.
- Process represents 41 percent.
- Business Environment represents 26 percent.
- Approximately 40 percent of items use predictive approaches.
- The remaining approximately 60 percent is divided between adaptive or agile and hybrid approaches.
- The examination contains 180 questions.
- 170 are scored and 10 are unscored pretest questions.
- The current examination time is 240 minutes.
- The 2026 update places increased emphasis on AI, sustainability, stakeholder engagement, value, outcomes, and business impact.
- Qualifying candidates generally need at least 35 hours of project-management training unless an applicable credit pathway applies.

These are time-sensitive facts. They must be reverified against PMI-ECO-2026 and PMI-PMP-CERTIFICATION-2026 immediately before public release.

## 3. Instructor method

Every lesson must use the following pattern:

1. Present a consequential decision.
2. Establish the authoritative source and its limitations.
3. Teach the underlying project-management concept.
4. Present verified facts or data.
5. Analyze a documented public case where appropriate.
6. Apply the concept to an original Obserra business scenario.
7. Explain the exam reasoning pattern.
8. Produce a workplace artifact.
9. Complete an original knowledge check.
10. Hand the learner to the Obserrian PMP Learning Coach for grounded remediation or extension.

The instructor must distinguish requirement, standard, guidance, recommended practice, internal policy, and professional judgment. ISO guidance is not law. A PMI task is not a statute. A GAO best-practice guide is not universally binding on private industry. A company procedure is not automatically a global standard. Learners must understand the source and authority of each statement.

# Part II — Module 1: PMP Exam Architecture and Modern Project Leadership

## Lesson 1 — PMP Eligibility, Application, Audit, and Exam Experience

### Teaching objective

The learner can identify the current education and experience pathways, count non-overlapping project leadership experience, distinguish projects from operations, preserve training evidence, and describe the examination experience without relying on unsupported advice.

### Core instruction

The application is the first evidence exercise in the PMP journey. Candidates must identify the applicable education path and document professional project leadership experience within the current eligibility window. Experience is counted in non-overlapping months. Parallel projects may demonstrate complexity, but they do not multiply calendar time.

A project is temporary work in a unique context intended to create value. Routine operations, repeated service delivery, and administrative work do not become projects simply because they involve deadlines, meetings, or coordination. The learner should identify a temporary objective, beginning and end, unique context, stakeholders, leadership responsibility, delivery decisions, and outcomes.

The 35-hour training requirement concerns formal instruction aligned to project-management topics. Books and practice examinations alone are not accepted as training. A completion certificate should identify the provider, course, learner, dates, and instructional hours. The learner should preserve the syllabus or objectives because PMI may audit training quality and alignment.

### Original business example

Maya Chen led a temporary identity-modernization program with six migration waves, a risk register, executive governance, budget constraints, and a transition to operations. She also ran weekly access reviews as part of an ongoing security process. The modernization program is clearly project work. The access review process is operational unless a separate temporary improvement project can be documented.

### Exam reasoning

When asked what a candidate or project manager should do next, first identify the authoritative requirement and the evidence gap. Do not choose an answer that invents qualifications, double-counts time, or relies on a title rather than verifiable work.

### Workplace artifact

PMP Eligibility Evidence Inventory.

### Source grounding

PMI-ECO-2026; PMI-PMP-CERTIFICATION-2026; PMI-PMP-EXAM-UPDATE-2026; PMI-ETHICS-2025.

## Lesson 2 — The 2026 Exam Content Outline: Domains, Tasks, and Enablers

### Teaching objective

The learner can explain the role of domains, tasks, and enablers and build a study map that covers the entire outline rather than memorizing isolated terms.

### Core instruction

The Examination Content Outline is a job-analysis-based map of what the certification exam assesses. Domains are broad knowledge and performance areas. Tasks describe responsibilities. Enablers illustrate work associated with a task but are not exhaustive. Every exam form addresses all tasks within a domain while preserving domain-level weighting.

The outline is not the same document as the PMBOK Guide. The outline describes the work and judgment tested. The PMBOK Guide provides principles, technical areas, processes, and practices that inform project management. Exam preparation must connect the two without treating either as a complete list of correct answers.

### Facts and data

- People: 33 percent.
- Process: 41 percent.
- Business Environment: 26 percent.
- Approximate delivery approach distribution: 40 percent predictive; 60 percent adaptive or agile and hybrid combined.
- The ECO was developed from job task analysis and validated against professional practice.

### Original learning model

Use the Obserra **DOMAIN–TASK–CONTEXT–ACTION** model:

1. Domain — What broad area is being tested?
2. Task — What responsibility is implicated?
3. Context — What facts, approach, authority, and constraints are present?
4. Action — What next action protects value, people, process, and accountability?

### Workplace artifact

PMI-ECO-2026.

## Lesson 3 — From Scope, Schedule, and Cost to Value and Outcomes

### Teaching objective

The learner can distinguish outputs, outcomes, benefits, and value and can recognize a project that is technically on plan but strategically unsuccessful.

### Core instruction

Traditional constraints remain important. A project cannot ignore budget, schedule, scope, quality, or risk. But meeting those constraints does not prove the work created value. The 2026 outline places explicit emphasis on stakeholder value, benefits, desired outcomes, business impact, and incremental delivery.

Outputs are what the project produces. Outcomes are changes enabled by those outputs. Benefits are measurable advantages realized by stakeholders or the organization. Value is the worth of those outcomes and benefits relative to effort, expense, risk, and stakeholder expectations.

### Documented public case

GAO-NASA-MAJOR-PROJECTS-2025 reported that NASA planned to invest approximately 74 billion dollars in major projects during fiscal year 2025. Four of 18 projects had cost overruns and three had schedule delays in the assessed year. GAO also reported that three of 53 historical projects accounted for almost half of total overruns in its analysis.

The instructional lesson is not that most projects fail. The source does not support that generalization. The lesson is that portfolio impact may be highly concentrated and that executives need to understand both individual project performance and the distribution of exposure across the portfolio.

### Original business scenario

Northstar Global Systems completes an ERP migration on time and within approved budget. Six months later, finance still uses spreadsheets, inventory accuracy has not improved, and business units have created workarounds. The output was delivered. The intended outcome was not achieved.

### Exam reasoning

When options include continuing to deliver planned outputs versus validating benefits and stakeholder value, examine whether the task calls for value-based delivery, expectation management, or business-environment alignment.

### Workplace artifact

Output–Outcome–Benefit–Value Chain.

### Source grounding

PMI-ECO-2026; GAO-NASA-MAJOR-PROJECTS-2025.

## Lesson 4 — Predictive, Adaptive, and Hybrid Thinking with Professional Ethics

### Teaching objective

The learner can tailor a delivery approach based on project characteristics and apply ethical values when pressure creates incentives to hide, bypass, or distort information.

### Core instruction

Predictive approaches emphasize defined scope, planned sequencing, baselines, and controlled change. Adaptive or agile approaches use short feedback cycles, evolving detail, prioritized work, and continuous stakeholder collaboration. Hybrid approaches combine elements deliberately.

No approach is automatically superior. The decision depends on uncertainty, stakeholder access, ability to deliver incrementally, regulatory constraints, technical risk, procurement structure, organizational capability, and the cost of change.

ISO-21502-2020 recognizes predictive, incremental, iterative, adaptive, hybrid, and agile approaches. This reinforces the principle that project practices should be tailored to context.

Professional ethics matters because approach selection can be manipulated. A sponsor may call work agile to avoid planning. A team may call a fixed commitment predictive to suppress learning. A manager may conceal forecast uncertainty to protect reputation. Responsibility, respect, fairness, and honesty require accurate communication of what is known, uncertain, authorized, and at risk.

### Original business scenario

The HELIX medical-device software project includes regulated design controls and a fixed external submission date, but the user experience and analytics capabilities are still evolving. A hybrid approach may preserve controlled evidence and formal gates for regulated components while using iterative development and frequent feedback for changeable features.

### Exam reasoning

Do not choose an approach based on fashion. Identify the characteristics, constraints, and value cadence. The strongest answer usually involves assessing needs and tailoring rather than declaring a universal method.

### Workplace artifact

### Source grounding

PMI-ECO-2026; ISO-21502-2020; PMI-ETHICS-2025.

# Part III — People Domain

## Module 2 — Vision, Leadership, and Team Performance

### Lesson 5 — Develop and Sustain a Common Vision

Teach the difference between a slogan and an actionable shared vision. The vision should connect purpose, stakeholders, value, constraints, and success. The instructor should demonstrate how misunderstandings arise when executives, customers, technical teams, and operations use the same words differently.

**Northstar scenario:** the AURORA cloud program is described as a migration by technology, a cost-reduction program by finance, a resilience program by the board, and a threat to local control by business units. The project manager must create and maintain a shared vision before planning detail can align.

**Artifact:** Project Vision and Success Narrative.

### Lesson 6 — Leadership Styles, Authority, and Situational Judgment

Teach that leadership style should respond to the situation. Directing may be appropriate during an immediate safety or containment event. Coaching and facilitation may be stronger when the team has expertise but needs alignment. Servant leadership is not absence of accountability. Delegation requires boundaries, resources, authority, and verification.

**Exam principle:** first understand the problem and engage the team before replacing people or escalating, unless urgency, ethics, safety, or authority requires immediate action.

**Artifact:** Leadership Response Matrix.

### Lesson 7 — Roles, Responsibilities, Empowerment, and Accountability

Clarify the difference between responsibility, accountability, authority, consultation, and information. Use original RACI-style teaching without claiming that one matrix solves all ambiguity. Show how adaptive teams distribute work while retaining clear product, governance, and risk accountability.

**Artifact:** Decision Rights and Responsibility Map.

### Lesson 8 — Team Diversity, Trust, Psychological Safety, and Performance

Teach diverse experience and perspective as controls against blind spots. The leader must create a climate where people can disclose uncertainty, errors, and emerging risk. Psychological safety does not remove standards or performance accountability.

**Business example:** a junior engineer identifies a dependency that threatens the schedule but remains silent because the status meeting rewards green reporting. The risk becomes visible only after integration fails.

**Artifact:** Team Working Agreement and Escalation Protocol.

### Lesson 9 — Performance Coaching and Structured Problem Solving

Separate performance symptoms from causes. Possible causes include role ambiguity, missing capability, unrealistic workload, unclear acceptance criteria, inaccessible decision makers, poor tooling, behavioral problems, and external dependencies. Coach and remove system impediments before assuming individual failure.

**Artifact:** Performance and Impediment Diagnostic.

## Module 3 — Stakeholders, Conflict, Communication, and Knowledge

### Lesson 10 — Stakeholder Identification, Analysis, and Engagement

Teach formal and informal power, interest, impact, influence, legitimacy, proximity, and urgency. Stakeholder analysis is dynamic. Hidden stakeholders often emerge through procurement, operations, regulators, unions, customers, or impacted communities.

**Artifact:** Stakeholder Influence and Impact Map.

### Lesson 11 — Align and Manage Expectations

Expectation management is not simply reporting. It requires surfacing incompatible definitions of success and negotiating trade-offs. The instructor should show how a sponsor can expect speed, a regulator evidence, a user simplicity, and operations stability from the same project.

**Artifact:** Expectation Alignment Record.

### Lesson 12 — Conflict Sources and Resolution Strategies

Conflict may arise from goals, roles, resources, priorities, data, methods, values, or interpersonal behavior. Collaboration is often strong but not universally appropriate. Direction may be necessary during an emergency. Avoidance may be temporary when emotions are high and facts incomplete. The learner must understand context.

**Artifact:** Conflict Analysis and Resolution Plan.

### Lesson 13 — Communication, Reporting, Feedback, and Knowledge Transfer

A status report should support decisions. It should identify actual status, forecast, variance, causes, impacts, decisions required, owners, confidence, and limitations. Knowledge transfer should begin before closure and should identify critical knowledge, not simply store files.

**Artifact:** Executive Status and Decision Brief.

# Part IV — Process Domain

## Module 4 — Tailoring, Integration, Scope, and Value

### Lesson 14 — Assess Complexity and Tailor the Development Approach

Use an original tailoring matrix that considers requirement stability, technology novelty, regulatory constraints, delivery cadence, stakeholder availability, procurement model, organizational maturity, and consequence of failure.

### Lesson 15 — Build and Maintain an Integrated Project Management Plan

Teach integration as active decision coordination, not document assembly. Scope decisions affect schedule, cost, resources, procurement, quality, risk, compliance, communications, and benefits.

GAO-SCHEDULE-GUIDE-2015 explains that an integrated schedule can help determine whether program milestones and completion dates are realistic and can show how change affects the program. GAO-COST-GUIDE-2020 provides a methodology for reliable cost estimates and emphasizes the importance of forecasts and updates.

**Artifact:** Integrated Plan Dependency Map.

### Lesson 16 — Scope, Requirements, Backlogs, and Work Decomposition

Teach scope definition, agreement, decomposition, acceptance, backlog refinement, and change. Distinguish product scope from project scope. Explain gold plating as unauthorized work even when well intentioned.

**Artifact:** Scope and Acceptance Baseline or Backlog Charter.

### Lesson 17 — Value-Based Delivery and Benefits

Teach incremental value, minimum viable learning, benefits ownership, measurement, and outcome review. Do not equate faster delivery with value if the delivered increment does not resolve a meaningful need.

**Artifact:** Benefits Measurement Plan.

### Lesson 18 — Estimation, Dependencies, Assumptions, and Data

Estimates are conditional forecasts. Teach analogous, parametric, bottom-up, three-point, relative, and expert methods at an application level. Emphasize assumptions, ranges, confidence, risk, and historical-data comparability.

**Artifact:** Estimate Basis and Uncertainty Record.

---

## Module 5 — Resources, Procurement, Finance, and Quality

### Lesson 19 — Resource Planning and Capacity

Plan people, skills, equipment, facilities, information, suppliers, and decision capacity. Resource loading must account for availability and competing work, not just nominal assignment.

### Lesson 20 — Procurement, Contracts, Negotiation, and Suppliers

Teach how scope clarity, risk allocation, incentives, uncertainty, and market conditions influence contract choice. Avoid teaching one contract type as universally best.

GAO-PROPULSION-MODULE-2001 reported that inadequate planning and design contributed to a project’s failure to meet performance, cost, and schedule goals. The later assessment defined success criteria, identified assumptions, and compared alternatives. The case supports disciplined planning and alternatives analysis, not a claim that procurement alone caused failure.

### Lesson 21 — Finance, Reserves, Forecasting, and Cost Control

Teach cost baseline, contingency reserve, management reserve, actual cost, commitment, forecast, and governance. A reserve is not hidden money. It should reflect known or unknown uncertainty and be controlled under defined authority.

### Lesson 22 — Quality, Compliance, Cost of Quality, and Improvement

Teach prevention, appraisal, internal failure, and external failure costs. Quality is planned into work and verified through evidence. ISO-10006-2017 provides quality-management guidance for projects across sectors and complexity levels.

---

## Module 6 — Schedule, Status, Artifacts, and Closure

### Lesson 23 — Schedule Networks, Dependencies, Critical Path, and Baselines

Teach logical relationships, milestones, calendars, leads, lags, float, critical path, and baseline integrity. A schedule with arbitrary dates and missing dependencies is not a reliable model.

GAO-SCHEDULE-GUIDE-2015 identifies ten best practices for a reliable, high-quality schedule. The course should summarize the practices without reproducing the guide’s copyrighted organization or tables.

### Lesson 24 — Schedule Risk, Variance, Forecasting, and Recovery

Teach schedule variance, forecast, risk analysis, crashing, fast tracking, scope negotiation, resequencing, and risk trade-offs. Do not conceal forecast changes to preserve a green status.

### Lesson 25 — Metrics, Status, Forecasts, and Artifacts

Distinguish measures from metrics, actuals from forecasts, and confidence from certainty. Use leading indicators where possible. Every executive metric should have a source, owner, refresh date, threshold, and action.

### Lesson 26 — Transition, Acceptance, Closure, and Knowledge

Closure requires acceptance, transition readiness, financial and procurement closure, resource release, unresolved-risk disposition, records, retrospective, and knowledge transfer. Premature closure transfers hidden risk to operations.

# Part V — Business Environment Domain

## Module 7 — Governance, Compliance, and Change

### Lesson 27 — Governance, Ethics, Escalation, and Success Metrics

Governance identifies who decides, under what authority, using what evidence, within what threshold, and with what accountability. It should be proportionate to risk and complexity.

### Lesson 28 — Compliance Categories, Evidence, and Consequences

A project may face legal, regulatory, contractual, security, privacy, safety, sustainability, accessibility, industry, and internal-policy obligations. The project manager should identify applicable requirements and owners rather than personally interpret every legal issue.

### Lesson 29 — Integrated Change Control and Configuration Integrity

A change request should be evaluated for impact, authority, dependencies, value, risk, compliance, funding, schedule, and benefits. Approved changes must update relevant plans and artifacts.

### Lesson 30 — Organizational and External Change

Projects operate within culture, strategy, market, technology, geopolitics, and regulation. The project manager should monitor external change and assess impact on scope, backlog, risk, value, and stakeholder expectations.

---

## Module 8 — Risk, Impediments, Improvement, and Readiness

### Lesson 31 — Risk Identification, Analysis, Response, and Communication

Teach threats and opportunities, qualitative and quantitative analysis, ownership, response strategies, triggers, residual risk, secondary risk, contingency, and monitoring. Risk communication should identify impact, likelihood, timing, confidence, owner, response, and decision need.

### Lesson 32 — Issues, Impediments, Root Causes, and Intervention

A risk becomes an issue when the uncertain event occurs or the condition exists. An impediment blocks or slows progress. The project manager should evaluate impact, prioritize, collaborate, intervene, and reassess.

### Lesson 33 — Continuous Improvement and Organizational Learning

A lesson is not learned merely because it is written down. The organization must change a process, standard, tool, control, training, decision, or behavior and then measure the result.

### Lesson 34 — AI, Sustainability, Security, and Modern Project Risk

The 2026 exam update adds AI and sustainability. The instructor must avoid treating AI as a generic productivity tool. AI projects introduce data, model, prompt, vendor, bias, security, privacy, explainability, oversight, drift, and accountability risks. Project managers need governance across design, development, deployment, operation, and retirement.

PMI-AI-PROJECT-WORK-2026 identifies AI business cases, tool selection, AI-specific risk management, ethics oversight, and emerging compliance as project leadership concerns. The course may use the announcement but may not reproduce the underlying standard without licensed access.

### Lesson 35 — Exam Strategy, Simulation, Remediation, and Capstone

Teach an original scenario-answering method:

1. Read the final sentence and identify the decision requested.
2. Identify domain and task.
3. Extract facts and remove assumptions.
4. Determine delivery approach and lifecycle context.
5. Identify current authority and stakeholders.
6. Eliminate answers that bypass analysis, collaboration, governance, or ethics.
7. Choose the action that addresses root cause and protects value.
8. Review why each distractor is weaker.

The final assessment must be original. It should measure application and judgment. The AI tutor must not reveal answers during the active assessment.

**Capstone:** create a 30-day PMP readiness plan and a workplace project-improvement plan using the learner’s domain performance.

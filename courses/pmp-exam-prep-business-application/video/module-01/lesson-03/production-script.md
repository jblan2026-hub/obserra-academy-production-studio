## Scene 3 — Four layers of project success — 02:45–06:15

**VISUAL**  
A four-level value model rises from a project platform.

### Layer 1 — Output

The product, service, capability, document, transition, or result the project produces.

Examples:

- A deployed enterprise platform.
- A completed facility.
- A released medical device.
- A new operating procedure.
- A trained workforce cohort.

### Layer 2 — Outcome

The observable change that follows from using or adopting the output.

Examples:

- Faster customer response.
- Reduced rework.
- Improved availability.
- Safer clinical workflow.
- More reliable regulatory evidence.

### Layer 3 — Benefit

A measurable improvement that contributes to organizational objectives.

Examples:

- Lower operating cost.
- Increased revenue.
- Reduced risk exposure.
- Improved mission readiness.
- Faster time to decision.

### Layer 4 — Value

The overall usefulness of the result to stakeholders after considering benefit, cost, risk, timing, sustainability, trade-offs, and unintended consequences.

**NARRATOR**  
A project manager does not unilaterally define organizational value. Value is established with sponsors, customers, product leaders, operational owners, users, regulators, and other stakeholders. The project manager helps make the relationship between work and value visible, measurable, and governable.

**ON-SCREEN WARNING**

> Completion is not the same as adoption. Adoption is not the same as benefit. Benefit is not the same as durable value.

- Business Environment — 26 percent.

**NARRATOR**  
The public July 2026 examination outline places explicit emphasis on outcomes, value, stakeholder engagement, and business impact. Those concepts do not belong to one isolated task. They affect leadership, planning, prioritization, quality, governance, change, risk, transition, and external-environment decisions.

Traditional controls remain important. Scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, and compliance provide necessary evidence. The exam, however, may ask what the project manager should do when those controls look acceptable but the intended outcome is weakening.

**EXAM REASONING FRAME**

1. Identify the intended outcome.
2. Compare the current evidence with the agreed success measures.
3. Determine who owns the benefit and who is affected.
4. Analyze the cause before prescribing a solution.
5. Select the next action within project authority.
6. Update plans, risks, communications, and governance as required.
7. Verify whether the intervention improves the outcome.

## Scene 5 — Fact and data sequence: NASA major-project portfolio — 08:40–12:15

**VISUAL**  
A source-labeled government portfolio visualization. The scene must use original Obserra graphics and must not reproduce GAO page layouts.

**SOURCE CARD**  
U.S. Government Accountability Office  
NASA: Assessments of Major Projects  
GAO-25-107591  
Published 1 July 2025  
Source ID: GAO-NASA-MAJOR-PROJECTS-2025

**NARRATOR**  
GAO reported that NASA planned to invest approximately 74 billion dollars in major projects in fiscal year 2025. In the assessed year, four of 18 major projects had cost overruns and three had schedule delays. GAO also reported that three of 53 historical projects accounted for almost half of total overruns in its analysis.

**ON-SCREEN FACTS**

- Approximately $74 billion planned major-project investment.
- Four of 18 assessed major projects had cost overruns.
- Three of 18 had schedule delays.
- Three of 53 historical projects represented almost half of total overruns in the cited analysis.

**NARRATOR**  
These facts illustrate portfolio concentration and the importance of evidence-based oversight. They do not prove that every overrun reflects poor project management, nor do they establish that one technique would have prevented the reported results. Major projects operate under technical uncertainty, external dependencies, funding decisions, policy changes, supply constraints, and mission-specific risks.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

- Public portfolio data is historical and context specific.
- Cost and schedule results do not alone measure mission value.
- Correlation does not establish a single cause.
- Learners must consult the official report before using figures in professional work.

**APPLICATION QUESTION**

> What portfolio decision would change if a small number of projects account for most exposure?

Learner options include intensified oversight, risk-based assurance, independent estimates, contingency decisions, or unchanged uniform governance. The feedback explains why concentrated exposure should influence governance attention without assuming failure.

## Scene 6 — The benefits chain — 12:15–15:20

**VISUAL**  
An animated benefits chain forms across the screen.

Business objective → capability → adoption → operational behavior → measurable outcome → benefit → strategic value.

A broken link appears between capability and adoption.

**NARRATOR**  
A benefits chain makes assumptions visible. If the project delivers a capability, what behavior must change? Who must use it? What operational process must be ready? What measure would show improvement? Who owns the measure after the project transitions?

**EXAMPLE — CYBERSECURITY TRANSFORMATION**

- Objective: reduce privileged-access exposure.
- Output: privileged-access management platform deployed.
- Adoption condition: administrators use approved vaulting and session controls.
- Outcome: unmanaged privileged credentials decline.
- Benefit: reduced likelihood and impact of credential misuse.
- Value: stronger resilience, auditability, and executive confidence relative to cost and disruption.

**NARRATOR**  
If the technology is deployed but administrators continue using unmanaged credentials, the output exists while the intended outcome remains weak.

**WORKBOOK PROMPT**

For one learner-selected project, document:

1. Output.
2. Required adoption behavior.
3. Outcome.
4. Benefit.
5. Value measure.
6. Benefit owner.
7. Earliest indicator that the chain is breaking.

## Scene 7 — Measures that support decisions — 15:20–18:20

**VISUAL**  
A dashboard transforms from activity-heavy metrics to outcome-oriented measures.

### Activity measures

- Meetings held.
- Stories completed.
- Training sessions delivered.
- Documents produced.
- Defects logged.

### Delivery measures

- Milestone completion.
- Lead time.
- Throughput.
- Schedule variance.
- Cost variance.
- Quality escape rate.

### Outcome and benefit measures

- User adoption.
- Process cycle time.
- Customer behavior.
- Risk reduction.
- Revenue or cost effect.
- Mission readiness.
- Regulatory evidence quality.

**NARRATOR**  
Activity measures can help manage work, but they should not be mistaken for value. A strong metric has a defined purpose, owner, source, calculation, frequency, threshold, limitation, and decision use.

**ON-SCREEN MODEL**

> Metric = measure + context + source + confidence + decision threshold.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**  
A training project reports 1,000 employees trained. The outcome measure shows that only 42 percent can apply the new process correctly. The strongest next action is not to celebrate completion or immediately repeat the same training. The project manager should analyze the learning and operating barriers, engage the responsible stakeholders, and adapt the intervention.

## Scene 8 — Original business scenario: Orion ERP — 18:20–22:05

**LABEL**  
Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**  
Orion Manufacturing completed an ERP implementation on time and within the approved budget. The system passed technical acceptance. Four weeks after go-live:

- 38 percent of targeted users log in weekly.
- Plants continue using local spreadsheets.
- Invoice cycle time has not improved.
- Support requests exceed the forecast by 210 percent.
- Finance expects the project team to close.
- Operations says process ownership was never clarified.

**QUESTION**

> What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Close the project because contractual acceptance is complete.  
B. Add more software features before investigating adoption.  
C. Analyze the outcome gap with the sponsor, operational owners, users, and benefits owner; identify causes; and agree on transition and remediation actions.  
D. Transfer all responsibility to operations without updating project records.

**NARRATOR**  
Option C is strongest. The project manager should not assume the cause, unilaterally extend scope, or declare success solely because the output was accepted. The first responsibility is to understand the gap, align accountability, and use governance to determine the appropriate next action.

**DECISION ANALYSIS**

- Facts: low adoption, unchanged cycle time, high support demand.
- Assumptions requiring validation: usability, training quality, process design, leadership reinforcement, data quality, role clarity.
- Authority: sponsor and governance determine material scope or transition changes.
- Evidence: adoption by role and site, workflow observation, support categories, process measures, stakeholder feedback.
- Verification: agreed measures improve after the intervention.

## Scene 9 — Exam traps and stronger reasoning — 22:05–24:45

**VISUAL**  
Five common traps appear as red project signals.

### Trap 1 — “The baseline is green, so close.”

Stronger reasoning: verify acceptance, transition readiness, benefits ownership, unresolved risk, and agreed outcome evidence.

### Trap 2 — “The customer is unhappy, so approve every requested change.”

Stronger reasoning: understand the need, assess value and impact, use the appropriate backlog or change process, and maintain governance.

### Trap 3 — “Benefits are outside the project manager’s responsibility.”

Stronger reasoning: benefit ownership may remain with the sponsor or business, but the project manager must connect delivery, transition, evidence, risks, and decisions to the intended outcome.

### Trap 4 — “More activity will create more value.”

Stronger reasoning: analyze the broken assumption or constraint before adding work.

### Trap 5 — “A public failure proves one method is superior.”

Stronger reasoning: use public evidence carefully, understand context and limitations, and avoid unsupported causal claims.

## Scene 10 — Knowledge check — 24:45–26:55

### Question 1

A project delivered the approved platform, but customer cycle time has not improved. What should the project manager do first?

A. Add new features.  
B. Confirm the output and then analyze the outcome gap with accountable stakeholders.  
C. Close immediately because scope is complete.  
D. Change the business case without sponsor involvement.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which statement best distinguishes an output from an outcome?

A. An output is always financial and an outcome is always technical.  
B. An output is what the project produces; an outcome is the observable change associated with using or adopting it.  
C. An output is measured after closure and an outcome is measured during planning.  
D. They are interchangeable.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

GAO reports that a small number of projects represented a large share of historical overruns. What is the most defensible lesson?

A. Every large project is poorly managed.  
B. Uniform oversight is always sufficient.  
C. Concentrated exposure can justify risk-based portfolio attention, while causes still require project-specific analysis.  
D. Cost variance alone proves that the project delivered no value.

**Correct response:** C.

## Scene 12 — Closing summary — 28:05–29:15

**NARRATOR**  
Project success requires disciplined delivery and disciplined attention to outcomes. Scope, schedule, cost, quality, and risk remain essential. They become more useful when connected to adoption, benefits, stakeholder value, and business impact.

Before choosing an answer, ask:

1. What result was the project intended to create?
2. What evidence shows whether that result is occurring?
3. Which assumption or link may be failing?
4. Who owns the benefit and the decision?
5. What is the strongest next action within authority?
6. How will the result be verified?

## Scene 1. Cold open: the same leader, three emergencies, 00:09 to 02:20

**VISUAL**

A project director receives three simultaneous messages.

### Message one

A newly formed team is preparing its first regulated product release. Roles are unclear, evidence requirements are misunderstood, and a compliance deadline is approaching.

### Message two

An experienced adaptive software team has a clear product goal but is blocked by a governance committee that has not clarified low risk release authority.

### Message three

A mature infrastructure team detects an active service failure affecting customers. The incident commander has already assumed authority and requests executive support to remove an external dependency.

A senior sponsor asks one question:

> How will you lead all three teams?

**NARRATOR**

One leadership style will not fit all three situations.

The new regulated team may require clear direction, explicit evidence standards, and close coaching. The experienced adaptive team may need authority clarified and an impediment removed, not additional task control. The incident team may need rapid support within a defined command structure, not a competing leader.

Leadership judgment begins by understanding what the situation requires and what authority the project manager actually holds.

## Scene 3. Leadership is not one personality, 03:05 to 06:20

**VISUAL**

A single project leader moves through several environments. The leader does not change identity. The leader changes behavior based on evidence.

**NARRATOR**

Leadership style is not a permanent personality label. It is the pattern of behavior used to influence direction, enable performance, protect people and value, and support accountable decisions.

A project manager may need to:

1. Direct when immediate clarity is required and decision authority is established.
2. Coach when a person or team needs capability, feedback, and guided practice.
3. Facilitate when diverse expertise must be integrated or conflict must be resolved.
4. Support when the team knows what to do but needs barriers, resources, or organizational protection removed.
5. Delegate when competence, context, authority, and risk controls support autonomous execution.
6. Escalate when the decision exceeds project authority, risk tolerance, legal boundaries, or ethical limits.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> The strongest leader does not maximize control. The strongest leader applies the right level of direction, support, and governance for the situation.

**NARRATOR**

The PMP exam often presents several actions that could eventually be useful. The candidate must identify the strongest first or next leadership action.

## Scene 4. The situational leadership diagnostic, 06:20 to 10:15

**VISUAL**

A seven factor diagnostic appears as a rotating decision model.

### Factor 1. Competence

Does the individual or team have the knowledge, skill, experience, and access required to perform the work?

### Factor 2. Commitment

Does the team understand the purpose, accept the responsibility, and have the confidence and motivation to act?

### Factor 3. Clarity

Are the objective, role, acceptance criteria, decision rights, and constraints understood?

### Factor 4. Urgency

How quickly must the decision or action occur to prevent harm or protect value?

### Factor 5. Risk and consequence

What could happen if the team acts incorrectly, acts late, or does not act?

### Factor 6. Authority

Who is authorized to decide, approve, accept risk, change scope, commit funding, or represent the organization?

### Factor 7. Environment

What regulatory, cultural, technical, contractual, geographic, political, and organizational conditions affect the choice?

**NARRATOR**

These factors should be analyzed together. High competence does not create authority. High urgency does not erase legal or ethical boundaries. Low confidence does not always mean low skill. A team can be technically capable but blocked by unclear governance or conflicting incentives.

**INTERACTIVE DIAGNOSTIC**

Learners classify four short cases by competence, clarity, urgency, authority, and risk. The system then compares their selected leadership behavior with an evidence based response.

## Scene 5. Direction without command and control reflexes, 10:15 to 12:45

**VISUAL**

A new project team receives a clear mission briefing. The project manager explains the objective, immediate priorities, evidence standard, decision boundaries, and escalation path.

**NARRATOR**

Direction is appropriate when ambiguity, inexperience, safety, compliance, or time pressure requires clear guidance. Direction becomes harmful when it suppresses expertise, creates unnecessary dependency, or substitutes the leader's preference for team judgment.

Effective direction includes:

1. Purpose and outcome.
2. Required action.
3. Decision authority.
4. Constraints and nonnegotiable requirements.
5. Evidence and completion criteria.
6. Time and escalation thresholds.
7. Confirmation of understanding.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A new medical device team is preparing a cybersecurity evidence package. The project manager does not merely say, “Work faster.” The manager clarifies the required evidence, owners, review sequence, regulatory dependencies, completion criteria, and escalation thresholds. Technical specialists retain authority over their professional analysis while the project manager directs the integrated delivery system.

## Scene 6. Coaching for capability and judgment, 12:45 to 15:20

**VISUAL**

A project manager reviews a risk register with an emerging team lead. Instead of correcting every entry, the manager asks structured questions.

**NARRATOR**

Coaching develops the learner's ability to diagnose and decide. It is useful when capability or confidence is developing and when the consequence of guided practice remains acceptable.

A strong coaching sequence may ask:

1. What objective are you protecting?
2. Which facts are known?
3. Which assumptions remain unverified?
4. Who owns the risk or decision?
5. What options are available?
6. Which evidence supports your recommendation?
7. How will you verify the result?

**NARRATOR**

Coaching is not withholding necessary information. If a team member is about to violate a safety, legal, security, or ethical boundary, the leader must intervene directly.

**ETHICS CONNECTION**

Responsibility includes developing capability without exposing stakeholders to unmanaged harm. Respect includes treating the learner as capable of growth. Fairness requires consistent expectations and evidence based feedback. Honesty requires clear communication about performance and limitations.

## Scene 7. Facilitation when expertise is distributed, 15:20 to 17:50

**VISUAL**

A hybrid project decision brings together product, security, legal, finance, operations, and engineering leaders. Each controls different evidence and authority.

**NARRATOR**

The project manager may not be the technical or legal decision maker. The leadership task is to create a process in which relevant expertise, evidence, constraints, and decision rights can be integrated.

Effective facilitation includes:

1. Defining the decision question.
2. Identifying who must participate.
3. Separating facts, assumptions, analysis, and preferences.
4. Establishing decision criteria.
5. Managing conflict and power imbalance.
6. Recording unresolved issues and dissent.
7. Confirming the accountable decision owner.
8. Documenting the decision and next actions.

**ORIGINAL BUSINESS EXAMPLE**

A product team wants to use a third party generative AI model. Security identifies data exposure risk. Legal identifies intellectual property uncertainty. Finance sees potential productivity value. Operations is concerned about support and vendor dependence.

The project manager should not unilaterally approve or reject the model. The manager facilitates an evidence based decision through the authorized governance structure.

## Scene 8. Support and servant leadership, 17:50 to 20:20

**VISUAL**

An experienced adaptive team is blocked by an external approval dependency. The project manager does not take over the backlog. Instead, the manager clarifies authority, accelerates the governance decision, secures resources, and protects the team from conflicting requests.

**NARRATOR**

Supportive and servant leadership focus on enabling the team to perform. The leader listens, removes impediments, protects focus, develops trust, and ensures that the team has access to decisions and resources.

Supportive leadership is not passive. It may require confronting a sponsor, negotiating resources, escalating a structural barrier, or stopping work that lacks authorization.

**ON SCREEN DISTINCTION**

> Serving the team means enabling responsible performance, not agreeing with every request or shielding the team from accountability.

## Scene 9. Delegation with boundaries, 20:20 to 23:05

**VISUAL**

A delegation contract appears with six fields.

1. Desired outcome.
2. Decision scope.
3. Constraints.
4. Resources.
5. Reporting and escalation thresholds.
6. Verification and acceptance.

**NARRATOR**

Delegation transfers defined decision or execution responsibility. It does not eliminate accountability.

A project manager should delegate when the person or team has sufficient competence, context, access, and authority, and when the risk is consistent with the defined boundaries.

Delegation fails when:

1. The outcome is unclear.
2. The delegate lacks necessary information or authority.
3. Constraints are hidden.
4. The leader continues to make every decision.
5. Escalation thresholds are absent.
6. The leader abandons oversight.

**EXAM TRAP**

An experienced team requests authority for a low risk decision. The project manager should not automatically approve it or automatically escalate it. First clarify the existing governance and decision rights. Empowerment must operate inside authorized boundaries.

## Scene 10. Documented public case: risk concentration and tailored oversight, 23:05 to 25:45

**VISUAL**

A source labeled government portfolio graphic appears.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
NASA: Assessments of Major Projects  
GAO 25 107591  
Published 1 July 2025  
Source ID: GAO NASA MAJOR PROJECTS 2025

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that NASA planned approximately 74 billion dollars in major project investment for fiscal year 2025. Four of 18 assessed major projects had cost overruns and three had schedule delays. In GAO's historical analysis, three of 53 projects accounted for almost half of total overruns.

This evidence supports a leadership and governance lesson. Uniform attention does not necessarily address concentrated exposure. Leadership should tailor oversight, expertise, intervention, and escalation to the risk and consequence of the work.

A low risk, experienced team may need autonomy. A high exposure project with unresolved evidence may require stronger independent review, clearer decision authority, and more frequent executive attention.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

The report does not evaluate a single universal leadership style. It does not prove that increased oversight alone would prevent overruns. Technical complexity, acquisition, funding, workforce, integration, and external conditions also matter.

## Scene 11. Original integrated scenario: Atlas Health Platform, 25:45 to 29:00

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Atlas Health is deploying a patient coordination platform.

1. The clinical workflow team is experienced and has clear product authority.
2. The data migration team is new and has misunderstood acceptance criteria.
3. The security team has identified a high impact identity risk that exceeds the project manager's acceptance authority.
4. The executive sponsor wants the project manager to direct every team personally to recover schedule.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do?

**OPTIONS**

A. Direct all teams using the same detailed task plan because the schedule is threatened.  
B. Delegate all decisions because each team has specialized expertise.  
C. Tailor leadership by team and risk: support the experienced clinical team, coach and direct the migration team where clarity is weak, and escalate the identity risk to the authorized decision owner.  
D. Ask the sponsor to manage the teams directly.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. It uses evidence, preserves expertise, applies proportionate direction, and respects decision authority.

A ignores differences in competence and context. B confuses expertise with authority and overlooks the new team. D abandons project leadership and may create competing command.

**FOLLOW UP**

The sponsor insists that the project manager accept the identity risk to protect the launch date.

The project manager should explain the evidence, document the risk and authority boundary, refuse to misrepresent approval, and escalate to the authorized governance body. Schedule pressure does not create risk acceptance authority.

## Scene 12. Common PMP leadership traps, 29:00 to 31:00

### Trap 1. Escalate every problem

Strong leaders facilitate resolution and act within authority before escalating. Escalation is appropriate when authority, risk, ethics, or organizational barriers require it.

### Trap 2. Empower without boundaries

Autonomy requires purpose, competence, context, authority, constraints, and verification.

### Trap 3. Direct experts because the manager is accountable

Accountability does not require micromanagement. Use expert judgment and preserve professional authority.

### Trap 4. Avoid intervention to protect team ownership

Leaders must intervene when safety, law, ethics, material risk, or clear performance failure requires action.

### Trap 5. Treat servant leadership as agreement

Serving the team means enabling responsible performance, not granting every preference.

### Trap 6. Accept a decision outside authority because it is urgent

Urgency may change timing and escalation. It does not automatically change legal or governance authority.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

An experienced adaptive team is waiting for clarification about whether it may release a low risk pilot. What should the project manager do first?

A. Authorize the release personally.  
B. Clarify the decision authority and expedite the required governance decision.  
C. Direct the team to redesign the pilot.  
D. Stop all work until the next monthly committee meeting.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

A new team lacks understanding of regulatory evidence requirements. Which leadership response is strongest?

A. Delegate the entire evidence package immediately.  
B. Provide clear direction on requirements and boundaries, then coach the team toward independent performance.  
C. Avoid intervention to encourage ownership.  
D. Ask the sponsor to complete the work.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A sponsor asks the project manager to accept a risk that exceeds the project manager's authority. What should the project manager do?

A. Accept the risk because the sponsor requested it.  
B. Hide the risk until after launch.  
C. Document the evidence and escalate the acceptance decision to the authorized owner.  
D. Transfer the risk to the team.

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 4

Which condition most strongly supports delegation?

A. The leader wants to reduce workload.  
B. The team has competence, context, authority, clear boundaries, and defined verification.  
C. The work is urgent.  
D. The sponsor has not responded.

**Correct response:** B.

## Closing summary, 31:00 to 32:10

**NARRATOR**

Effective project leadership is situational and accountable. Diagnose competence, commitment, clarity, urgency, risk, authority, and environment before choosing how to lead.

Direct when clarity and consequence require it. Coach to develop judgment. Facilitate distributed expertise. Support experienced teams. Delegate with boundaries. Escalate when the decision exceeds authority or ethical limits.

On the PMP exam, avoid reflexes. Do not automatically command, delegate, or escalate. Identify the controlling facts, select a proportionate action, preserve accountability, and verify the result.

> Diagnose. Tailor. Empower. Govern. Verify.

## Cold open: recovery by pressure

A delayed project responds by adding overtime, parallelizing testing, deferring documentation, and asking the supplier to accelerate without a contract change. The dashboard improves for two weeks. Defects rise, staff leave, rework increases, and the supplier submits a claim.

**NARRATOR**

Urgency does not make every acceleration responsible. Recovery begins with diagnosis and an executable decision, not pressure alone.

## Recovery diagnostic

1. Define the gap and consequence.
2. Confirm baseline, actual, forecast, and source quality.
3. Identify immediate containment needs.
4. Analyze technical, process, resource, dependency, decision, supplier, and behavioral causes.
5. Separate symptoms from causes.
6. Identify decisions beyond project authority.
7. Develop options.
8. Model integrated impact and residual risk.
9. Recommend and obtain authorization.
10. Implement, verify, and learn.

## Corrective-action distinctions

### Correction

Fixes an observed nonconformance or immediate problem.

### Corrective action

Addresses cause to reduce recurrence.

### Preventive action

Addresses a potential cause before the problem occurs.

### Workaround

A temporary response used when the preferred response is unavailable or an unplanned problem requires immediate handling.

### Change

An authorized modification to scope, plan, baseline, contract, control, or other governed element.

**NARRATOR**

A workaround should not silently become the permanent operating model. It needs ownership, risk, duration, review, and exit criteria.

## Recovery option set

1. Resequence work.
2. Fast track selected critical-path activities.
3. Crash selected critical-path activities.
4. Add or develop capability.
5. Remove an impediment or accelerate a decision.
6. Change supplier or contract through authorized channels.
7. Reduce, defer, or simplify scope through governance.
8. Change delivery approach.
9. Increase automation or test capability.
10. Adjust milestone or benefit timing.
11. Pause for evidence, safety, or quality.
12. Stop the project or phase.

Each option should show expected benefit, cost, time, disruption, quality, risk, stakeholder, contractual, and residual effects.

## Documented public case: planning and design recovery

GAO reported that an initial Space Station propulsion-module project failed to meet performance, cost, and schedule goals largely because fundamental planning and execution processes were not followed. A later assessment established success criteria, identified assumptions, and compared alternatives before recommending a design.

**Lesson:** Recovery should revisit the problem definition, success criteria, assumptions, and alternatives rather than merely accelerate the existing plan.

**Limitations:** Historical government context with multiple technical and organizational factors. The case does not prove one recovery method guarantees success.

## Original scenario: Meridian Recovery Decision

Meridian is ten weeks late on a regulated product release.

1. The critical-path supplier interface is incomplete.
2. Quality rework consumes 25 percent of capacity.
3. The sponsor requests weekend work and parallel validation.
4. Legal approval is still pending.
5. The supplier contract does not authorize accelerated work.
6. One low-value feature can be deferred.

**Strongest action:** Complete an integrated recovery analysis. Protect legal and validation gates, address defect causes, engage procurement on supplier options, model scope deferral and schedule scenarios, and obtain an authorized recovery decision.

The approved plan defers the low-value feature, adds independent integration testing, changes the supplier sequence through contract authority, resolves two design causes, and establishes twice-weekly evidence reviews. Overtime is limited and monitored rather than treated as the primary strategy.

## Stop and replan criteria

Recovery may not be credible when:

1. Required outcomes or obligations cannot be met.
2. Remaining value does not justify cost and risk.
3. Critical evidence is unavailable.
4. The solution is no longer viable.
5. Stakeholder or environmental conditions materially changed.
6. Funding or authority is withdrawn.
7. Safety, ethics, or compliance prevents continuation.

The project manager presents evidence and options to the authorized decision owner rather than continuing because of sunk cost.

## Common exam traps

1. Add overtime before root-cause analysis.
2. Fast track everything.
3. Hide variance until a complete solution exists.
4. Use reserve or change contracts without authority.
5. Correct the symptom but not the cause.
6. Preserve all scope while claiming schedule recovery.
7. Continue because substantial money has already been spent.

## Knowledge checks

1. A defect is fixed but the process cause remains. **Implement and verify corrective action.**
2. A temporary workaround is used. **Document risk, owner, duration, review, and exit.**
3. Recovery requires supplier acceleration outside contract. **Engage authorized procurement and contracting roles.**
4. Remaining value no longer justifies cost and risk. **Present stop, pivot, or replan options to governance.**

## Obserrian PMP Coach handoff

## Closing

Recovery is an integrated decision. Diagnose the cause, contain immediate harm, compare executable options, protect guardrails, obtain authority, and verify whether the corrective action changes the outcome.

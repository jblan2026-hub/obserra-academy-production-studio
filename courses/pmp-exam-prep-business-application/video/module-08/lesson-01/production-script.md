## Cold open: low probability, unacceptable consequence

A project team ranks a critical supplier failure as low because probability is 10 percent. If the event occurs, the product cannot launch for nine months. The team accepts the risk because its scoring matrix produces “medium.” No executive risk owner sees the decision.

**NARRATOR**

A risk score is an aid, not authority. Low probability does not make a catastrophic consequence acceptable. Risk management connects uncertainty to objectives, ownership, responses, decisions, and evidence.

## Risk statement quality

Use a cause-event-effect structure when useful:

> Because a single supplier owns the proprietary component, there is a possibility that supplier disruption will delay integration, resulting in launch delay, additional cost, and customer impact.

Separate:

1. Cause.
2. Uncertain event or condition.
3. Effect on objective.

Avoid records such as “supplier risk” or “schedule may slip” without decision-relevant detail.

## Qualitative analysis

Assess dimensions appropriate to the project:

1. Probability.
2. Impact by objective.
3. Urgency and proximity.
4. Detectability.
5. Manageability.
6. Connectivity and cascading effect.
7. Strategic or stakeholder consequence.
8. Evidence confidence.

A probability-impact matrix should define scales and decision thresholds. It should not conceal severe consequence, correlated exposure, or weak evidence.

## Quantitative analysis

### Probability-impact exposure

A 20 percent probability of a 500,000-dollar cost impact has simplified expected monetary exposure of:

> 0.20 × 500,000 = 100,000 dollars.

### Opportunity

A 40 percent probability of 150,000 dollars in savings has expected monetary value of:

> 0.40 × 150,000 = 60,000 dollars of expected benefit.

### Decision tree example

Option A costs 50,000 dollars and reduces a 30 percent probability of a 400,000-dollar loss to 10 percent.

Before response exposure:

> 0.30 × 400,000 = 120,000 dollars.

After response exposure plus response cost:

> 0.10 × 400,000 + 50,000 = 90,000 dollars.

Simplified expected improvement:

> 120,000 − 90,000 = 30,000 dollars.

**NARRATOR**

This calculation supports analysis. It does not capture every consequence, risk appetite, timing, correlation, or stakeholder effect.

## Threat responses

1. Avoid: remove the threat or change the objective or approach.
2. Mitigate: reduce probability or impact.
3. Transfer: shift defined financial or performance responsibility through insurance, contract, or another mechanism; accountability and residual risk may remain.
4. Accept: acknowledge and monitor, with active contingency or passive acceptance as appropriate.
5. Escalate: move to a program, portfolio, enterprise, legal, regulatory, or executive owner when outside project authority.

## Opportunity responses

1. Exploit: make the opportunity occur.
2. Enhance: increase probability or benefit.
3. Share: partner with a party better able to realize it.
4. Accept: take advantage if it occurs without active pursuit.
5. Escalate: move to the appropriate owner when outside project authority.

## Original scenario: Titan Supplier Concentration

Titan plans a critical launch. One supplier provides a proprietary component.

1. Failure probability is estimated at 10 percent.
2. Failure would delay launch nine months and affect 80 million dollars in expected value.
3. A qualified alternate costs 2 million dollars and needs six months to prepare.
4. The project risk matrix rates the risk medium.
5. The project manager can spend only 250,000 dollars without executive approval.

**Strongest action:** Reassess the severe consequence and evidence, quantify scenarios, develop alternate-source, inventory, redesign, insurance, schedule, and acceptance options, and escalate the risk and investment decision to the authorized enterprise owner.

Do not accept the risk solely because the matrix label is medium.

## Risk monitoring

Monitor:

1. Trigger conditions.
2. Response-action completion.
3. Probability and impact changes.
4. Actual events and issues.
5. Residual and secondary risk.
6. Aggregate and correlated exposure.
7. Schedule and cost reserve.
8. Emerging external conditions.
9. Owner engagement.
10. Response effectiveness.

When a risk occurs, manage the event as an issue and execute or adapt the planned response.

## Common exam traps

1. Choose response from risk score alone.
2. Transfer risk and assume accountability disappears.
3. Accept a severe risk without authorized ownership.
4. Close a risk after the response action without measuring residual exposure.
5. Monitor a realized risk instead of managing the issue.
6. Treat opportunities as optional positive comments rather than managed uncertainty.
7. Keep enterprise risk inside the project.

## Knowledge checks

1. 20 percent probability of 500,000-dollar impact. **EMV = 100,000 dollars.**
2. Risk exceeds project authority. **Escalate with evidence and options.**
3. Response creates a new risk. **Record and manage secondary risk.**
4. Risk event occurs. **Execute the response and manage it as an issue.**

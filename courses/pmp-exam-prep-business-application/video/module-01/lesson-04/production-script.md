## Scene 1 — Cold open: one program, three kinds of work — 00:08–02:10

**VISUAL**  
A medical technology company is preparing a connected product launch.

Workstream A: regulated hardware validation with fixed evidence and approval milestones.  
Workstream B: cloud analytics with evolving user needs and rapid feedback.  
Workstream C: enterprise deployment requiring procurement, security, training, and regional rollout.

A program executive asks:

> “Are we predictive or agile?”

**NARRATOR**  
The question assumes that one label must govern every part of the work. The hardware team faces controlled design, verification, and regulatory evidence. The cloud team needs discovery, experimentation, feedback, and incremental learning. The enterprise rollout depends on contractual milestones, local readiness, security controls, and phased adoption.

The strongest answer may not be one method. It may be a governed system that uses different approaches deliberately and integrates them transparently.

## Scene 4 — Predictive evidence — 05:20–08:10

**VISUAL**  
A network schedule grows from approved scope and controlled dependencies.

**PREDICTIVE INDICATORS**

- Scope and solution are sufficiently understood.
- Work can be decomposed and sequenced.
- Dependencies and approval points can be modeled.
- Baselines support control and forecasting.
- Contractual, regulatory, safety, or physical constraints require formal evidence.
- Change impact must be evaluated before authorization.

**NARRATOR**  
Predictive delivery is not simply “waterfall.” It is a deliberate approach that emphasizes advance planning, baselines, integrated control, and formal change when the nature of the work supports those practices.

**EXAMPLE**  
A facility electrical upgrade has defined engineering requirements, permit dependencies, long-lead equipment, inspection points, and a fixed outage window. A reliable integrated schedule, resource plan, risk analysis, and controlled change process are essential.

**EXAM REASONING**

When a predictive scenario presents a proposed change, the strongest answer usually evaluates impact, uses the approved decision process, updates the baseline after authorization, communicates the decision, and monitors implementation. It does not automatically reject change or implement it without analysis.

## Scene 5 — Adaptive evidence — 08:10–11:05

**VISUAL**  
A product goal drives a prioritized backlog. Small increments move through discovery, build, review, and learning loops.

**ADAPTIVE INDICATORS**

- Requirements or solution details are uncertain.
- Early feedback can change priorities.
- Value can be delivered incrementally.
- A cross-functional team can make bounded local decisions.
- Frequent review reduces the cost of being wrong.
- Work is prioritized rather than fully fixed in advance.

**NARRATOR**  
Adaptive delivery does not eliminate planning. It changes the planning horizon, feedback cadence, decision model, and artifact set. Product goals, release intentions, backlog ordering, quality criteria, risk thresholds, and governance still matter.

**EXAMPLE**  
A customer analytics product has a clear desired outcome but uncertain feature usefulness. The team tests small increments with authorized users, measures behavior, learns, and reorders work based on evidence.

**ETHICAL BOUNDARY**  
Calling work agile does not justify ignoring privacy, security, accessibility, contractual, or regulatory obligations. Speed does not erase accountability.

## Scene 6 — Hybrid evidence — 11:05–14:10

**VISUAL**  
Three delivery streams connect to a common governance spine.

- Predictive infrastructure milestones.
- Adaptive digital-product increments.
- Phased organizational rollout.

**NARRATOR**  
Hybrid delivery combines approaches intentionally. The important question is not whether the word hybrid appears in a plan. The question is whether the organization has defined interfaces, decision rights, dependencies, integration points, evidence, and common outcome measures.

**HYBRID DESIGN QUESTIONS**

1. Which work benefits from fixed sequencing and baselines?
2. Which work benefits from experimentation and frequent feedback?
3. Where do the workstreams exchange inputs or create dependencies?
4. Which risks and decisions require common governance?
5. How will integrated status and value be measured?
6. Who resolves conflicts between local optimization and enterprise outcomes?

**ISO CONTEXT**  
The public ISO 21502 description recognizes predictive, incremental, iterative, adaptive, hybrid, and agile approaches across different project contexts. The course paraphrases the public description and does not reproduce copyrighted clauses.

## Scene 7 — Approach-selection model — 14:10–17:25

**VISUAL**  
A decision matrix evaluates the work against eight dimensions.

| Dimension | Predictive signal | Adaptive signal | Hybrid signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requirement stability | High | Low or evolving | Mixed |
| Solution uncertainty | Lower | Higher | Different by component |
| Feedback availability | Periodic | Frequent | Varies by stream |
| Value cadence | Major releases | Incremental | Multiple cadences |
| Regulatory evidence | Formal and staged | Embedded and continuous where permitted | Shared evidence architecture |
| Physical dependencies | Strong | Limited | Present in selected streams |
| Team autonomy | Structured roles | Empowered bounded decisions | Local autonomy plus common governance |
| Contract model | Defined deliverables | Flexible capacity or outcome model | Multiple contract types |

**NARRATOR**  
No single row determines the answer. The project manager evaluates the pattern, the organization’s capabilities, stakeholder needs, risk, and governance requirements.

**WORKBOOK EXERCISE**

For a learner-selected project:

1. Score the eight dimensions.
2. Recommend an approach by workstream.
3. Identify integration points.
4. Define decision rights.
5. Record the risk of choosing the wrong approach.
6. Identify evidence that would cause the approach to change.

## Scene 8 — Professional ethics as an operating control — 17:25–20:25

**VISUAL**  
Four gold pillars appear inside the project governance model.

- Responsibility.
- Respect.
- Fairness.
- Honesty.

**NARRATOR**  
PMI identifies responsibility, respect, fairness, and honesty as core ethical values. Ethics is not a separate chapter used only for obvious misconduct. It affects estimates, status reporting, conflict, procurement, stakeholder treatment, data use, risk disclosure, change decisions, and claims about project progress.

### Responsibility

Own decisions within your authority, disclose limits, escalate appropriately, and protect the integrity of project records.

### Respect

Listen to affected stakeholders, treat disagreement professionally, and avoid using authority to silence legitimate evidence.

### Fairness

Apply decision criteria consistently, disclose conflicts of interest, and do not manipulate access or information for favored parties.

### Honesty

Report what the evidence supports. Do not hide forecast deterioration, mislabel unfinished work, or present assumptions as facts.

**ON-SCREEN**

> The delivery approach does not change the ethical standard.

## Scene 9 — Original scenario: the “agile” launch date — 20:25–23:35

**LABEL**  
Obserra original synthetic scenario.

**SCENARIO**  
A software team uses two-week iterations. An executive publicly announces a fixed launch date before architecture and security work is understood. The product owner directs the project manager to keep the dashboard green because “agile teams adapt.” Current evidence shows:

- Critical security work is not estimated.
- Two external integrations are untested.
- User feedback changes the highest-priority workflow.
- The team has no agreed release readiness criteria.
- The announced date is now unlikely without reducing scope or increasing risk.

**QUESTION**

> What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Keep the dashboard green until the team formally misses the date.  
B. Direct the team to work overtime because the date was publicly announced.  
C. Analyze the evidence with the product owner and team, define release options and risks, communicate an honest forecast through governance, and obtain an authorized decision.  
D. Remove security work from the backlog because it does not create visible customer value.

**NARRATOR**  
Option C is strongest. The project manager protects transparency, value, safety, and decision authority. The answer is not anti-agile. It is disciplined adaptive planning with ethical reporting.

**DECISION RECORD**

- Known facts.
- Uncertainty.
- Options.
- Scope and value effect.
- Security and operational risk.
- Schedule forecast.
- Decision authority.
- Verification criteria.

## Scene 10 — Original scenario: regulated hybrid delivery — 23:35–26:20

**LABEL**  
Obserra original synthetic scenario.

**SCENARIO**  
A connected medical product includes regulated hardware, cloud software, an AI-supported analytics feature, and hospital deployment. Hardware verification has formal evidence gates. Software requirements will evolve through clinician feedback. Hospital deployment depends on contracts, cybersecurity review, training, and local readiness.

**QUESTION**

> Which delivery strategy is strongest?

**OPTIONS**

A. Use one predictive lifecycle for every workstream because the product is regulated.  
B. Use one adaptive lifecycle for every workstream because software requirements evolve.  
C. Design a hybrid operating model with workstream-specific approaches, common risk and evidence governance, controlled integration points, and shared outcome measures.  
D. Allow each team to choose independently without common governance.

**NARRATOR**  
Option C is strongest. Regulation does not automatically require one uniform method, and adaptive delivery does not remove controlled evidence. The integrated model must make interfaces and accountabilities explicit.

**LIMITATION**  
This scenario is educational. Real medical product development requires organization-specific regulatory, quality, clinical, cybersecurity, and legal expertise.

## Scene 11 — Common exam traps — 26:20–28:20

### Trap 1 — Choose the method from one keyword

“Backlog” does not prove the organization is operating adaptively. “Baseline” does not mean change is prohibited. Analyze behavior and governance.

### Trap 2 — Agile means no documentation

Strong adaptive teams maintain decision-useful artifacts and evidence appropriate to risk and obligation.

### Trap 3 — Predictive means the plan never changes

Predictive control includes change analysis and authorized baseline updates.

### Trap 4 — Hybrid means more process

Hybrid should integrate different work appropriately, not multiply ceremonies without value.

### Trap 5 — Protect the deadline by hiding uncertainty

Ethical project leadership communicates evidence, options, limitations, and implications before the organization loses decision time.

## Scene 12 — Knowledge check — 28:20–30:05

### Question 1

A team delivers frequent increments, but every decision requires executive approval and user feedback is collected only at the end. What is the strongest conclusion?

A. The work is adaptive because iterations are short.  
B. The work is predictive because executives are involved.  
C. The label and cadence do not prove the operating model; analyze decision rights, feedback, prioritization, and value delivery.  
D. The project manager should rename the method immediately.

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 2

A change is proposed on a predictive project. What should the project manager do first?

A. Reject it because the scope is baselined.  
B. Implement it immediately to satisfy the stakeholder.  
C. Evaluate impact and use the approved change decision process.  
D. Ask the team to absorb it without updating records.

**Correct response:** C.

### Question 3

A sponsor asks the project manager to hide a deteriorating forecast until after a board meeting. What is the strongest response?

A. Comply because the sponsor owns the project.  
B. Delay reporting but document the request privately.  
C. Report the evidence, uncertainty, options, and implications through the appropriate governance channel while acting professionally and within policy.  
D. Publish the information externally.

**Correct response:** C.

## Scene 14 — Closing summary — 30:50–31:35

**NARRATOR**  
Choose the approach from the work, not from fashion. Tailor planning, feedback, governance, evidence, and decision rights to uncertainty, value, risk, and obligation. Integrate different workstreams deliberately. Report what the evidence supports, especially when pressure makes transparency uncomfortable.

Before answering an exam scenario, ask:

1. How is the work actually being delivered?
2. What is known and what remains uncertain?
3. Which approach best protects value and learning?
4. What governance or evidence is required?
5. Who has authority?
6. What is the ethical responsibility?
7. How will the outcome be verified?

**ON-SCREEN**

> Tailor the method. Preserve the truth. Govern the outcome.

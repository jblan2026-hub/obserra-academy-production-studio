## Scene 3. The financial architecture, 03:25 to 07:10

**VISUAL**

Nine financial terms appear.

### Cost estimate

A forecast of resources and cost required for defined work under stated assumptions.

### Budget

Authorized financial provision for the project or component.

### Cost baseline

The time-phased approved budget used for performance measurement, commonly excluding management reserve.

### Funding

Money made available according to organizational timing and authority.

### Actual cost

Cost incurred for work performed.

### Commitment

A contractual or organizational obligation that may not yet appear as an actual payment.

### Forecast

Current prediction of total cost or future cash need using actual performance and known conditions.

### Contingency reserve

Budget for identified uncertainty or risk within the controlled project baseline, subject to the organization's definitions and authority.

### Management reserve

Budget held for unknown or broader uncertainty outside the cost baseline and controlled by designated management authority.

**NARRATOR**

Organizations use these terms differently. The project manager must follow the approved financial system and authority model.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> Having reserve in the total budget does not mean the project manager may spend it without authorization.

## Scene 5. Reserves and risk, 10:50 to 14:20

**VISUAL**

Three risks appear with probability, impact, response, and expected monetary value.

Risk A:

30 percent probability of 200,000 dollars impact.

> Expected monetary value = 0.30 times 200,000 = 60,000 dollars.

Risk B:

20 percent probability of 500,000 dollars impact.

> Expected monetary value = 0.20 times 500,000 = 100,000 dollars.

Opportunity C:

40 percent probability of 150,000 dollars savings.

> Expected monetary value = 0.40 times negative 150,000 = negative 60,000 dollars when modeled as cost reduction.

Net simplified expected monetary value:

> 60,000 + 100,000 minus 60,000 = 100,000 dollars.

**NARRATOR**

Expected monetary value can inform quantitative risk analysis and contingency. It is not automatically the correct reserve amount. Risks may be correlated, impact distributions may be asymmetric, response costs matter, and organizational risk appetite affects decisions.

Reserve use should identify:

1. Which risk or uncertainty occurred.
2. Which amount is authorized.
3. Who approves.
4. How baseline and forecast change.
5. What evidence is retained.
6. Whether residual risk remains.

## Scene 6. Earned-value foundations, 14:20 to 19:35

**VISUAL**

A project at a status date has:

Budget at completion, BAC: 1,000,000 dollars.  
Planned value, PV: 500,000 dollars.  
Earned value, EV: 450,000 dollars.  
Actual cost, AC: 600,000 dollars.

### Schedule variance

> SV = EV minus PV.

> 450,000 minus 500,000 = negative 50,000 dollars.

A negative schedule variance indicates less budgeted work was completed than planned at the status date.

### Cost variance

> CV = EV minus AC.

> 450,000 minus 600,000 = negative 150,000 dollars.

A negative cost variance indicates the earned work cost more than its budgeted value.

### Schedule performance index

> SPI = EV divided by PV.

> 450,000 divided by 500,000 = 0.90.

### Cost performance index

> CPI = EV divided by AC.

> 450,000 divided by 600,000 = 0.75.

**NARRATOR**

An index below 1.0 indicates unfavorable performance under these measures.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

Schedule performance index is not a calendar forecast and loses usefulness near project completion. Earned-value analysis depends on a credible baseline, accurate progress measurement, and suitable work structure.

## Scene 7. Forecasting at completion, 19:35 to 23:35

**VISUAL**

Several estimate-at-completion formulas appear with assumptions.

### If current cost efficiency is expected to continue

> EAC = BAC divided by CPI.

> 1,000,000 divided by 0.75 = 1,333,333 dollars.

### If remaining work is expected to perform at the original budgeted rate

> EAC = AC plus BAC minus EV.

> 600,000 + 1,000,000 minus 450,000 = 1,150,000 dollars.

### If both cost and schedule performance are expected to affect remaining work

> EAC = AC plus BAC minus EV, divided by CPI times SPI.

> Remaining budgeted work = 550,000.  
> CPI times SPI = 0.75 times 0.90 = 0.675.  
> 550,000 divided by 0.675 is approximately 814,815.  
> EAC is approximately 1,414,815 dollars.

**NARRATOR**

Different formulas answer different assumptions. The project manager should not select the formula that produces the preferred result.

A bottom-up estimate to complete may be strongest when the original assumptions are no longer valid.

### Variance at completion

> VAC = BAC minus EAC.

Using the CPI-only forecast:

> 1,000,000 minus 1,333,333 = negative 333,333 dollars.

### To-complete performance index against BAC

> TCPI = BAC minus EV, divided by BAC minus AC.

> 550,000 divided by 400,000 = 1.375.

**NARRATOR**

A TCPI of 1.375 would require much stronger future cost efficiency than current performance. The project should assess whether that expectation is realistic.

## Scene 8. Cash flow, commitments, and funding limits, 23:35 to 26:05

**VISUAL**

A project remains under total budget while monthly cash requirements exceed available funding.

**NARRATOR**

Financial management should distinguish total cost from timing.

A project can fail financially because:

1. Supplier milestones concentrate payment.
2. Funding arrives after payment is due.
3. Currency or inflation changes cost.
4. Commitments are not visible in actuals.
5. Dual operation extends longer than planned.
6. Capital and operating classifications affect approval.
7. Benefit delays weaken the business case.
8. Reserve authority is misunderstood.

The project manager should maintain a cash-flow and commitment view appropriate to the organization.

## Scene 10. Original enterprise scenario: Horizon Platform Forecast, 28:55 to 33:15

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Horizon has a budget at completion of 2 million dollars.

At the status date:

PV = 1,200,000 dollars.  
EV = 1,000,000 dollars.  
AC = 1,250,000 dollars.

The sponsor says the project is “only” 250,000 dollars over the amount earned and asks the project manager to preserve the original forecast.

**CALCULATIONS**

SV = 1,000,000 minus 1,200,000 = negative 200,000 dollars.  
CV = 1,000,000 minus 1,250,000 = negative 250,000 dollars.  
SPI = 1,000,000 divided by 1,200,000 = approximately 0.83.  
CPI = 1,000,000 divided by 1,250,000 = 0.80.  
CPI-based EAC = 2,000,000 divided by 0.80 = 2,500,000 dollars.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Keep the original forecast because the sponsor requested it.  
B. Report actual and earned-value evidence, analyze causes and remaining work, develop realistic forecast scenarios, and present options through financial governance.  
C. Use management reserve without approval.  
D. Stop all work immediately.

**NARRATOR**

Option B is strongest.

**FOLLOW UP**

A bottom-up review finds that one supplier defect is resolved and future efficiency should improve, but dual operation adds 180,000 dollars. The project presents multiple forecasts, assumptions, reserve needs, scope options, and benefit effects. Governance approves a revised forecast and corrective plan.

## Scene 11. Common PMP finance traps, 33:15 to 35:15

### Trap 1. Select the most favorable forecast formula

Choose the forecast based on credible assumptions and current evidence.

### Trap 2. Treat contingency as unallocated spending

Link reserve use to identified uncertainty and authority.

### Trap 3. Use management reserve without approval

Respect the designated control owner.

### Trap 4. Report actual cost without commitments

Include contractual and forecast obligations.

### Trap 5. Protect the baseline by hiding the forecast

Communicate current evidence and use governance.

### Trap 6. Treat CPI or SPI as complete project health

Interpret them with scope, quality, risk, outcome, and calendar evidence.

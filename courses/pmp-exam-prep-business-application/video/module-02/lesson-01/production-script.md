## Scene 3 — What a common vision is — 02:55–06:15

**VISUAL**  
A vision model assembles from six layers.

### Purpose

Why the project exists and which problem or opportunity it addresses.

### Future state

What will be materially different when the project succeeds.

### Stakeholder value

Who should receive value and what value means to them.

### Success evidence

Which observable outcomes will show that the future state exists.

### Boundaries and principles

Which conditions, obligations, and non-negotiable constraints shape decisions.

### Shared commitment

How leaders and teams will use the vision when priorities conflict.

**NARRATOR**  
A common vision is not a marketing slogan. It is a shared model of purpose and future state that helps people make consistent decisions.

A useful vision is short enough to remember, specific enough to guide trade-offs, and measurable enough to test.

**ON-SCREEN DISTINCTIONS**

- Vision describes the intended future and purpose.
- Outcomes describe observable changes.
- Benefits describe measurable advantages.
- Scope defines approved boundaries and deliverables.
- Strategy explains how the organization intends to compete or fulfill its mission.
- The project plan organizes the work required to move toward the future state.

**NARRATOR**  
These artifacts support each other, but they are not interchangeable.

## Scene 4 — The common-vision task on the exam — 06:15–08:45

**VISUAL**  
An original People-domain map appears. The words are paraphrased rather than copied from PMI tables.

**NARRATOR**  
The July 2026 Examination Content Outline expects project leaders to develop and sustain a common vision, communicate it, assess whether stakeholders understand it, identify the causes of misunderstanding, and keep the vision aligned as conditions change.

The exam may not use the phrase “common vision.” Instead, it may show:

- Stakeholders using different success measures.
- Teams optimizing local goals at the expense of the project.
- A sponsor and customer expecting different outcomes.
- A project team that cannot explain why the work matters.
- A strategic change that makes the original direction obsolete.
- A technically complete project that no longer supports the business objective.

**EXAM LENS**

1. Is the problem a lack of shared purpose or a detailed scope dispute?
2. Which stakeholders define or are affected by success?
3. What facts reveal misunderstanding?
4. Should the project manager facilitate alignment, investigate causes, update the vision, or escalate a strategic decision?
5. How will shared understanding be verified?

## Scene 5 — A vision that guides decisions — 08:45–12:20

**VISUAL**  
The weak cloud-migration sentence returns:

> Migrate selected enterprise workloads to the cloud.

It expands into a decision-useful vision.

**ORIGINAL OBSERRA VISION EXAMPLE**

> Create a resilient, secure, and financially sustainable digital operating environment that allows business units to release customer capabilities faster while preserving regulatory obligations, data protection, and accountable local decision authority.

**NARRATOR**  
This version does not solve every design question. It establishes the future state and the principles that should guide design decisions.

The team then defines outcome measures:

- Release lead time.
- Service availability.
- Unit operating cost.
- Privileged-access exposure.
- Recovery performance.
- Business-unit adoption.
- Compliance evidence quality.

**NARRATOR**  
A vision without evidence can become rhetoric. Evidence without a vision can become disconnected measurement. Strong project leadership connects both.

**DECISION EXAMPLE**

A team proposes a rapid migration method that reduces schedule time but increases operational fragility and privileged access.

The common vision makes the trade-off visible. The fastest option may conflict with resilience, security, and accountable authority. The project manager should facilitate analysis and route the decision through the appropriate governance process.

## Scene 6 — Documented public case: defining success before selecting a design — 12:20–16:00

**VISUAL**  
A clearly labeled documentary sequence appears:

> DOCUMENTED PUBLIC CASE · U.S. GOVERNMENT ACCOUNTABILITY OFFICE

Original Obserra graphics depict a space-station propulsion capability decision. No agency logo is used as an endorsement device.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
Space Station: Inadequate Planning and Design Led to Propulsion Module Project Failure  
GAO-01-633  
Published 20 June 2001  
Source ID: GAO-PROPULSION-MODULE-2001

**NARRATOR**  
GAO reported that an initial propulsion-module project did not meet performance, cost, and schedule goals, and that fundamental planning and execution processes were not followed. A subsequent assessment established success criteria, identified design assumptions, and compared alternatives before recommending a design.

**ON-SCREEN LESSONS**

- Define success before selecting a solution.
- Make assumptions visible.
- Compare alternatives against common criteria.
- Separate mission need from a preferred design.
- Use evidence to align stakeholders and governance.

**NARRATOR**  
This case is useful because it shows the relationship between vision, success criteria, assumptions, and alternative analysis. It does not prove that a common vision alone guarantees project success. Technical, funding, policy, acquisition, and organizational factors also matter.

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

- Historical government context.
- Not directly equivalent to every commercial project.
- The report supports planning lessons but not a single-cause conclusion.
- Learners should review the official report before using the case professionally.

## Scene 7 — Diagnosing misunderstanding — 16:00–19:15

**VISUAL**  
A diagnostic matrix displays six causes of apparent disagreement.

### Different desired outcomes

Stakeholders genuinely want different futures.

### Different definitions

Stakeholders use the same words but mean different things.

### Incomplete evidence

Stakeholders are interpreting different facts or data.

### Hidden constraints

Legal, political, financial, technical, or personal concerns have not been surfaced.

### Misaligned incentives

Local performance measures reward behavior that conflicts with project value.

### Trust and power

Stakeholders may not believe the process is fair, safe, or responsive.

**NARRATOR**  
The project manager should not treat every disagreement as a communication failure. Sometimes the vision is unclear. Sometimes it is understood but rejected. Sometimes the conflict requires a sponsor or governance decision because stakeholder interests are genuinely incompatible.

**DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS**

- Ask each stakeholder to describe success in their own words.
- Ask what they believe will change and for whom.
- Ask which evidence would prove success or failure.
- Ask which constraints cannot be violated.
- Ask what decision they believe they own.
- Ask what risk they believe others are overlooking.

---

## Scene 8 — Verifying shared understanding — 19:15–21:30

**VISUAL**  
The project manager uses five verification techniques.

1. **Teach-back** — Stakeholders explain the vision in their own words.
2. **Scenario test** — Teams use the vision to decide between competing options.
3. **Measure test** — Stakeholders identify which outcomes show success.
4. **Boundary test** — Teams identify decisions that would violate the vision.
5. **Traceability test** — Major scope and backlog items map to an intended outcome.

**NARRATOR**  
Sending a vision statement does not prove understanding. Attendance at a workshop does not prove alignment. Verification requires observable evidence.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

When asked to choose between rapid feature delivery and stronger evidence controls, three teams make different decisions. That result shows that the vision or decision principles are not yet sufficiently shared.

## Scene 9 — Sustaining the vision through change — 21:30–24:10

**VISUAL**  
External conditions strike the project model:

- New regulation.
- Supply disruption.
- Strategic acquisition.
- Cyber incident.
- AI capability change.
- Customer behavior shift.
- Funding reduction.

**NARRATOR**  
Sustaining a common vision does not mean refusing to change it. The project manager should monitor whether the vision remains aligned with strategy, value, stakeholder needs, and the external environment.

### When the vision remains valid

Update plans, scope, sequencing, and tactics while preserving the intended future state.

### When the vision is no longer valid

Engage the sponsor and governance authority to reassess the project purpose, benefits, and continuation decision.

### When stakeholders disagree about changing it

Facilitate evidence-based analysis, document trade-offs, identify decision authority, and escalate the strategic choice rather than imposing a personal preference.

**ETHICS CONNECTION**

Responsibility requires accurate information and accountable decisions. Respect requires listening to affected stakeholders. Fairness requires transparent criteria. Honesty requires reporting when the original vision no longer fits the evidence.

## Scene 10 — Original enterprise scenario: Northstar Cloud — 24:10–27:25

**LABEL**  
Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO FACTS**

- Sponsor goal: reduce infrastructure cost by 25 percent.
- Product goal: cut release lead time by half.
- Security goal: eliminate unmanaged privileged access.
- Operations goal: maintain 99.99 percent availability.
- Business-unit concern: preserve local approval authority.
- Current charter: migrate 70 percent of workloads within 18 months.
- Teams are selecting architectures using different success criteria.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Ask the sponsor to select the single most important metric and discard the others.  
B. Continue detailed planning because the migration percentage is already approved.  
C. Facilitate a common-vision and success-criteria session, expose conflicts and constraints, and route unresolved strategic trade-offs to the accountable governance authority.  
D. Allow each workstream to define success independently.

**NARRATOR**  
Option C is strongest. The project manager should align the future state and measures before allowing local plans to harden around conflicting assumptions.

A may oversimplify value. B confuses scope with shared purpose. D institutionalizes fragmentation.

**FOLLOW-UP DECISION**

After alignment, the sponsor reduces the target migration percentage but preserves cost, speed, resilience, security, and authority outcomes. The project manager should update the charter, plans, measures, stakeholder communications, and traceability rather than treating the original percentage as untouchable.

## Scene 11 — Common exam traps — 27:25–29:10

### Trap 1 — Escalate before facilitating

Escalation may be necessary, but the project manager should first understand the disagreement and facilitate alignment when the decision remains within the team’s ability to resolve.

### Trap 2 — Assume communication equals understanding

A presentation or email does not prove a shared vision. Use observable verification.

### Trap 3 — Protect the charter language after the strategy changes

A charter is governed, not sacred. Analyze impact and use authorized change when purpose or strategic context changes.

### Trap 4 — Solve a vision problem with more detailed scheduling

Additional detail cannot correct incompatible definitions of success.

### Trap 5 — Create consensus by suppressing dissent

A common vision should integrate material concerns, not hide them. Ethical leadership surfaces risk and records unresolved disagreement.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

Stakeholders use different measures of project success and planning is stalled. What should the project manager do first?

A. Ask the sponsor to issue a directive.  
B. Facilitate alignment on the project purpose, future state, outcomes, and measurable success.  
C. Complete the schedule independently.  
D. Record every disagreement as an issue and continue.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which technique best verifies that stakeholders understand a common vision?

A. Confirm that they received the presentation.  
B. Ask them to explain how the vision would guide a difficult project trade-off.  
C. Count workshop attendance.  
D. Obtain a generic statement of support.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A new regulation makes the original project purpose less valuable. What should the project manager do?

A. Preserve the original vision because it was approved.  
B. Hide the change until the next phase.  
C. Analyze the strategic impact with stakeholders and route any change to the accountable governance authority.  
D. Rewrite the vision without sponsor involvement.

**Correct response:** C.

## Closing summary — 29:10–30:15

**NARRATOR**  
A common vision is a shared, decision-useful understanding of purpose and future state. It connects stakeholders, outcomes, value, boundaries, and evidence.

Develop it by surfacing different definitions of success. Sustain it by testing understanding, tracing decisions to purpose, and reassessing alignment when conditions change.

On the exam, do not solve a vision problem with more detailed planning. Diagnose the misunderstanding, engage the right stakeholders, facilitate alignment, respect decision authority, and verify that people can use the vision to make coherent choices.

**ON-SCREEN**

> Shared purpose. Visible trade-offs. Accountable decisions.

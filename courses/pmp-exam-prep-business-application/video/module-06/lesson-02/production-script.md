## Cold open: the green project that needed an executive decision

A dashboard reports 94 percent complete, on budget, and green. The evidence shows that the remaining six percent includes regulatory approval, supplier integration, operational transition, and customer acceptance. The completion percentage is mathematically correct and operationally misleading.

**NARRATOR**

Project status is not a color or one number. It is an evidence-based explanation of current condition, forecast, uncertainty, consequence, and decision need.

## Learning objectives

Learners will be able to:

1. Define decision-useful project metrics.
2. Separate actual, baseline, forecast, target, and threshold.
3. Evaluate source quality, freshness, confidence, and limitations.
4. Select leading and lagging indicators.
5. Maintain accessible and controlled project artifacts.
6. Prepare an executive status and decision briefing.
7. Recognize vanity metrics and misleading aggregation.

**Sources:** PMI ECO 2026, ISO 21502, GAO Schedule Guide, GAO Cost Guide, GAO NASA Major Projects 2025 and 2026.

## Status architecture

A complete status view contains:

1. Outcome and value forecast.
2. Scope and backlog condition.
3. Schedule baseline, actual, critical path, and forecast.
4. Cost baseline, actual, commitments, and estimate at completion.
5. Quality, defects, acceptance, and assurance.
6. Risks, issues, dependencies, and decisions.
7. Resources, suppliers, and capacity.
8. Compliance, security, safety, and sustainability.
9. Stakeholder, adoption, and transition readiness.
10. Confidence, limitations, and missing data.

## Metric contract

Every significant metric should identify:

1. Name and purpose.
2. Definition and calculation.
3. Source system and owner.
4. Observation date and reporting period.
5. Scope and population.
6. Threshold and target.
7. Confidence and limitations.
8. Decision or action supported.
9. Access, classification, and retention.
10. Validation and change history.

**NARRATOR**

Without a metric contract, teams may use the same label for different measures or compare values that do not share a denominator, time period, or scope.

## Leading and lagging indicators

Leading indicators provide early evidence about conditions that may influence future results. Examples include unresolved decisions, critical-resource availability, test readiness, risk-response completion, backlog aging, and supplier lead-time trend.

Lagging indicators describe results already experienced. Examples include cost variance, milestone delay, defects found after release, customer complaints, incidents, and realized benefits.

Use both. Leading indicators without outcome verification can reward activity. Lagging indicators alone may arrive too late for effective action.

## Aggregation and concentration risk

A portfolio average can conceal a critical project. A 98 percent control-completion rate can conceal the missing control that protects the highest-risk asset. A green overall schedule can conceal one near-critical supplier path.

GAO reported that a small number of historical NASA projects accounted for a large share of portfolio overruns. The instructional lesson is to display concentration, distribution, and material exceptions rather than only averages.

**Limitations:** The NASA portfolio is a complex government aerospace context and does not prescribe one dashboard design.

## Artifact governance

Project artifacts may include charters, plans, roadmaps, schedules, backlogs, cost models, risk records, contracts, quality evidence, decisions, requirements, tests, reports, and lessons.

Artifacts should have:

1. Authoritative location.
2. Owner.
3. Version and effective date.
4. Access control.
5. Approval status.
6. Source and dependencies.
7. Retention and disposition.
8. Change history.
9. Relationship to current decisions.

A document stored in several locations without an authoritative version is an operational risk.

## Executive decision briefing

A decision-ready report answers:

1. What changed?
2. Why does it matter?
3. Which evidence supports the conclusion?
4. What is the current forecast?
5. What remains uncertain?
6. Which options exist?
7. What are the cost, schedule, value, risk, and stakeholder effects?
8. Who is authorized to decide?
9. What is recommended and why?
10. How will implementation be verified?

## Original scenario: Atlas Enterprise Dashboard

Atlas reports 92 percent completion and green status. The remaining work includes a critical regional approval, a supplier security retest, and production failover. The completion metric weights every task equally. The sponsor asks the project manager to preserve green status.

**Strongest action:** Explain the metric limitation, show milestone and risk concentration, update the forecast, identify the required decision and owners, and preserve the source evidence. Do not change the underlying data or conceal material exposure.

## Common exam traps

1. Communicate more instead of fixing the metric or source.
2. Use one status measure for every audience.
3. Protect green status by hiding forecast or uncertainty.
4. Treat percent complete as objective without a measurement rule.
5. Change an artifact without version control or authority.
6. Report activity rather than outcome or decision relevance.

## Knowledge checks

1. A metric lacks source and definition. **Validate the metric contract before relying on it.**
2. Portfolio average is stable but one project drives most exposure. **Show concentration and material exception.**
3. Actual performance differs from baseline. **Update forecast, analyze cause, and use governance.**
4. Executives need a decision. **Provide evidence, options, authority, recommendation, and limitations.**

A project status system converts current evidence into responsible decisions. Define metrics, protect authoritative artifacts, display concentration and uncertainty, forecast honestly, and make the required action visible.

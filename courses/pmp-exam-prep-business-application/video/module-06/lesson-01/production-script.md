## Scene 3. The schedule model, 03:20 to 06:50

A schedule should identify:

1. Activities and milestones.
2. Logical relationships.
3. Durations and calendars.
4. Resources and constraints.
5. Leads, lags, and assumptions.
6. Critical path and float.
7. External dependencies.
8. Risk and uncertainty.
9. Baseline and current forecast.
10. Status date and actual progress.
11. Ownership and update cadence.
12. Decision and change authority.

**NARRATOR**

The schedule is an analytical model. It should show how the project is expected to unfold and how a change in one area affects the rest.

## Scene 4. Activity definition and sequencing, 06:50 to 10:35

Activities should be derived from approved scope and deliverables and should include integration, review, approval, transition, and project-management work.

Logical relationships include finish-to-start, start-to-start, finish-to-finish, and less commonly start-to-finish. Dependencies should represent real work and constraints. Leads and lags should not conceal activities or accountability.

**EXAMPLE**

Instead of using a 30-day lag for regulatory review, model preparation, submission, review, questions, response, and approval when those activities and owners are decision-relevant.

## Scene 5. Critical path and float, 10:35 to 15:05

A network contains:

Path A: 7 + 9 + 6 = 22 days.  
Path B: 5 + 8 + 5 = 18 days.  
Path C: 4 + 12 + 7 = 23 days.

Path C is the current critical path at 23 days. If project completion is constrained to 23 days, Path A has approximately one day of total float and Path B has approximately five days, subject to calendars and constraints.

**NARRATOR**

The critical path is the longest-duration path through the current logical network. It can change after progress, delay, rework, resource shifts, or logic changes.

Free float measures how long an activity may slip without delaying its immediate successor. Total float measures how long it may slip without delaying the project completion or constrained milestone.

## Scene 6. Constraints, leads, lags, and calendars, 15:05 to 18:15

Hard constraints can override network logic and create misleading float. Use them only when a real contractual, regulatory, physical, or authorized date requires them.

Calendars affect duration and sequence. A 10-day activity on a five-day workweek spans differently than one using continuous operations. Regional holidays, maintenance windows, supplier calendars, and limited test environments matter.

**EXAM TRAP**

Do not solve a delayed schedule by adding arbitrary constraints or reducing durations without evidence.

## Scene 7. Schedule compression, 18:15 to 21:20

### Fast tracking

Perform work in parallel that was originally sequential. It may reduce duration but increase coordination, rework, quality, and risk.

### Crashing

Add cost or resources to shorten critical-path work when the activity can be accelerated.

**NARRATOR**

Analyze compression at the activity and project level. Crashing a noncritical activity does not shorten the project. Fast tracking may create more rework than time saved.

**WORKED EXAMPLE**

Activity X on the critical path can be reduced by four days for 40,000 dollars. Cost slope:

> 40,000 divided by 4 = 10,000 dollars per day.

Compare this with other critical-path options, risk, contract, and value before deciding.

## Scene 8. Schedule risk analysis, 21:20 to 24:20

A schedule risk review examines:

1. Uncertain durations.
2. Correlated risks.
3. External approvals.
4. Resource concentration.
5. Supplier lead times.
6. Rework and quality.
7. Merge bias and path convergence.
8. Calendar and access constraints.
9. Critical and near-critical paths.
10. Confidence in milestone dates.

A deterministic date should not be presented as certain when the schedule depends on unresolved uncertainty.

## Scene 9. Government guidance and performance evidence, 24:20 to 27:35

**SOURCE CARD**

GAO Schedule Assessment Guide, GAO 16 89G.

GAO describes ten best practices for developing and maintaining reliable high-quality schedules, including capturing all activities, sequencing work, assigning resources, establishing durations, confirming the critical path, testing schedule risk, updating actual progress, and maintaining a baseline.

**SOURCE CARD TWO**

GAO NASA Major Projects, GAO 26 108556.

GAO's July 2026 assessment reported cumulative portfolio schedule delay of approximately 14 years across assessed major projects. This evidence reinforces the need for integrated schedule logic, current forecasting, and transparent escalation.

**LIMITATIONS**

Government guidance and complex aerospace portfolios do not prescribe one schedule detail level for every project.

## Scene 10. Original scenario: Citadel Global Release, 27:35 to 32:20

Citadel plans a global release in 90 days.

1. The schedule omits two regional approvals.
2. One test environment is shared by three teams.
3. A supplier interface is four weeks late.
4. Training is scheduled before the final workflow is stable.
5. The sponsor requests fast tracking.
6. The current critical path is not verified.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do first?

A. Fast track every activity.  
B. Add a mandatory 90-day constraint.  
C. Complete and validate the integrated schedule, identify the critical and near-critical paths, analyze resources and risks, and then present recovery options.  
D. Remove the supplier activity.

**Correct response:** C.

The analysis shows that regional approval and integration testing form the critical path. The project can sequence one approval earlier, procure a temporary test environment, and fast track selected training design without training users on unstable procedures. Governance approves a revised forecast and risk response.

## Scene 11. Updating and controlling the schedule, 32:20 to 34:25

At each status cycle:

1. Set the status date.
2. Record actual start, finish, remaining duration, and progress evidence.
3. Update logic, calendars, resources, and risks.
4. Recalculate critical and near-critical paths.
5. Compare baseline and forecast.
6. Analyze variance and cause.
7. Develop options.
8. Obtain authorized changes.
9. Communicate decisions.
10. Verify corrective action.

## Scene 12. Common PMP schedule traps, 34:25 to 36:10

1. Treating a target date as a calculated schedule.
2. Compressing noncritical work.
3. Assuming overtime or unlimited resources.
4. Hiding missing work in lags.
5. Preserving a baseline while concealing the forecast.
6. Reporting percent complete without remaining-duration evidence.
7. Ignoring near-critical paths and schedule risk.

## Knowledge check package

1. Which path is critical when paths are 22, 18, and 23 days? **23 days.**
2. Which compression method adds resources or cost to shorten critical-path work? **Crashing.**
3. What should happen before fast tracking? **Analyze critical-path effect, coordination, quality, rework, and risk.**
4. Why should the schedule be updated with actual and remaining-duration evidence? **To maintain a credible current forecast and decision model.**

A credible schedule is an integrated, resource-aware, risk-informed model. Build complete work and logic. Calculate critical path and float. Analyze uncertainty. Update with actual evidence. Use authorized change and recovery decisions.

## Scene 3. Five layers of scope, 03:20 to 06:55

**VISUAL**

Five scope layers appear.

### Outcome

The change or value the project intends to create.

### Product scope

The features, functions, characteristics, services, or capabilities of the product or result.

### Project scope

The work required to deliver, transition, and close the project and enable the intended outcome.

### Requirement

A documented condition or capability needed by a stakeholder, contract, regulation, product, process, or system.

### Work item

A decomposed unit of work used to plan and execute delivery.

**NARRATOR**

These layers are connected but not interchangeable.

A project may deliver a training system as product scope, but project scope also includes research, content development, legal review, accessibility, media production, testing, entitlement integration, learner support, release, and evidence retention.

**ON SCREEN WARNING**

> A feature list is not the complete project scope. A task list is not the intended outcome.

## Scene 4. Scope discovery and requirement quality, 06:55 to 10:40

**VISUAL**

A requirement record appears with nine fields.

1. Identifier.
2. Source and stakeholder.
3. Need or obligation.
4. Requirement statement.
5. Rationale and value.
6. Priority.
7. Acceptance criteria.
8. Dependencies and constraints.
9. Traceability and change history.

**NARRATOR**

A strong requirement is clear enough to analyze, build, test, and accept. It identifies the need and the evidence that will demonstrate satisfaction.

Requirement sources may include:

1. Customer and user needs.
2. Business strategy.
3. Contracts.
4. Regulation and policy.
5. Safety, security, privacy, accessibility, and quality obligations.
6. Technical interfaces.
7. Operational support.
8. Data and reporting needs.
9. Transition and benefit requirements.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

Weak requirement:

> The system must be easy to use.

Stronger requirement structure:

> A first-time authorized user must be able to complete the defined enrollment workflow using keyboard-only navigation and a supported screen reader, with zero critical accessibility defects and a median completion time under the approved target during representative usability testing.

**NARRATOR**

The exact criteria must be approved for the project. The example shows testability, not a universal standard.

## Scene 5. Decomposition and the work breakdown structure, 10:40 to 14:40

**VISUAL**

A project outcome decomposes into deliverables, subdeliverables, work packages, and activities.

**NARRATOR**

Decomposition divides scope into manageable components while preserving the whole.

A work breakdown structure should represent 100 percent of the approved project scope at the level used for control. It should avoid overlap and include project-management and transition work, not only product construction.

**ON SCREEN DECOMPOSITION QUESTIONS**

1. What deliverables are required?
2. What acceptance evidence is needed?
3. Which work is internal or external?
4. What integration and transition work is easy to overlook?
5. Which component needs separate ownership or control?
6. Is the level of detail sufficient for estimating and monitoring?
7. Would further detail improve a decision or only create administrative burden?

**NARRATOR**

The 100 percent rule is a planning principle, not a command to create the maximum number of tasks.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

“Deploy platform” is decomposed into environment readiness, identity integration, data migration, controls, testing, operating procedures, training, support, transition, and acceptance.

## Scene 6. Adaptive product backlog and boundaries, 14:40 to 18:15

**VISUAL**

A product goal sits above a prioritized backlog. Items move based on value, risk, dependency, learning, and readiness.

**NARRATOR**

In adaptive delivery, the backlog represents the evolving product work. It is not an ungoverned request list.

A healthy backlog should contain:

1. Items aligned to the product goal.
2. Sufficient description and acceptance criteria for near-term work.
3. Priority based on value, risk, urgency, dependency, and learning.
4. Clear ownership.
5. Traceability for controlling obligations.
6. Refinement at an appropriate cadence.
7. Removal or deferral of low-value work.
8. Visibility into technical, security, quality, and operational work.

**ON SCREEN DISTINCTION**

> Backlog flexibility allows priority and detail to evolve. It does not grant authority to ignore contracts, controls, budgets, or governance.

**EXAM TRAP**

The project manager should not add every stakeholder request directly to the next iteration. The product owner or authorized prioritization role should assess value, risk, dependencies, and product goals.

## Scene 7. Acceptance criteria and definition of done, 18:15 to 21:30

**VISUAL**

An item moves through two verification layers.

### Acceptance criteria

Conditions specific to the deliverable, requirement, feature, or item.

### Definition of done or completion standard

Shared quality and completion conditions that apply to work, such as review, testing, documentation, security, accessibility, integration, and evidence.

**NARRATOR**

Acceptance criteria reduce misunderstanding and make completion observable.

The project should identify:

1. Who accepts.
2. Which evidence is required.
3. When acceptance occurs.
4. Which exceptions are permitted.
5. How defects and nonconformance are handled.
6. Whether acceptance transfers ownership or risk.
7. Which records must be retained.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A reporting feature is not done because the screen displays data. Completion may require approved definitions, data lineage, access controls, testing, performance, accessibility, documentation, support, and stakeholder acceptance.

## Scene 8. Requirements traceability, 21:30 to 24:20

**VISUAL**

A traceability chain connects:

Need or obligation.  
Requirement.  
Design.  
Work item.  
Test.  
Evidence.  
Acceptance.  
Outcome measure.

**NARRATOR**

Traceability helps verify that requirements are implemented, tested, accepted, and connected to purpose.

It is especially important when the project has:

1. Regulation or contractual obligations.
2. Safety or security requirements.
3. Many suppliers or interfaces.
4. Long life cycles.
5. High change volume.
6. Complex testing and evidence.
7. Multiple releases or configurations.

**LIMITATION CARD**

Traceability can become expensive and ceremonial if it records links that no decision or assurance process uses. Tailor depth to risk and obligation.

## Scene 9. Scope change analysis, 24:20 to 27:25

**VISUAL**

A change request passes through an impact network.

1. Outcome and value.
2. Product and project scope.
3. Schedule and release.
4. Cost and funding.
5. Resources and capacity.
6. Quality and testing.
7. Risk and opportunity.
8. Security, privacy, safety, and compliance.
9. Procurement and contracts.
10. Stakeholders and communication.
11. Benefits and transition.
12. Technical debt and future options.

**NARRATOR**

Scope creep occurs when scope expands without appropriate analysis, authorization, and updates.

Gold plating occurs when the team adds unapproved features or quality beyond the agreed scope, often with good intent. It can create risk, cost, test, support, contract, or acceptance problems.

Clarification is not always a change. Correcting a defect is not always new scope. A newly discovered obligation may require change but cannot be dismissed as optional scope creep.

**ON SCREEN DECISION PATH**

Clarify.  
Classify.  
Analyze.  
Decide.  
Update.  
Communicate.  
Implement.  
Verify.

## Scene 10. Guidance-based dependency example, 27:25 to 29:40

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
GAO Schedule Assessment Guide, GAO 16 89G

**NARRATOR**

GAO guidance emphasizes that an integrated schedule should include the activities necessary to accomplish the work and logically connect dependencies.

Scope decomposition supports this objective. If major work is omitted from the scope model, it may also be omitted from estimates, resources, schedule logic, risk, and acceptance.

**ORIGINAL APPLICATION**

A program includes software build but omits data cleansing, user acceptance, transition, and operational support from the work structure. The schedule appears achievable because essential work is missing.

The project manager should complete the scope and integration model before relying on the forecast.

## Scene 11. Original enterprise scenario: Helix Reporting Change, 29:40 to 34:20

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Helix is launching a global analytics platform in four weeks.

A customer requests one additional sensitive-data field.

1. Engineering estimates two hours of development.
2. The field changes the data classification.
3. Legal must assess retention and regional use.
4. Security must update access and monitoring.
5. Analytics must approve the definition.
6. Test cases and training must change.
7. The supplier interface is outside contract scope.
8. The product owner believes the feature has high customer value.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Approve the feature because development is only two hours.  
B. Reject it because the launch is close.  
C. Clarify the desired outcome, analyze the integrated impact, identify decision authority, and route the change through the approved product and project governance process.  
D. Ask engineering to add it without telling other teams.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. Effort is only one dimension of impact.

**FOLLOW UP**

Governance decides to defer the field to the next release and offers an existing report as an interim solution. The backlog, customer communication, contract discussion, risk, and roadmap are updated.

The project preserves value without bypassing data, legal, security, and supplier controls.

## Scene 12. Common PMP scope traps, 34:20 to 36:20

### Trap 1. Accept every stakeholder request

Analyze and prioritize through the authorized process.

### Trap 2. Reject every request as scope creep

Clarify whether it is a defect, obligation, omission, preference, or new scope.

### Trap 3. Gold plate to delight the customer

Unapproved additions can create risk and acceptance problems.

### Trap 4. Treat the backlog as project scope without transition work

Include integration, evidence, training, operations, and closure.

### Trap 5. Decompose into tasks before understanding deliverables

Begin with outcomes and deliverables, then work.

### Trap 6. Treat acceptance as a signature only

Define evidence, authority, conditions, exceptions, and ownership transfer.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A customer requests a small feature that changes data classification and supplier scope. What should the project manager do?

A. Add it because coding is minimal.  
B. Analyze integrated impact and obtain the authorized scope and product decision.  
C. Reject it automatically.  
D. Let the engineer decide.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which statement best distinguishes product scope from project scope?

A. Product scope describes product characteristics; project scope includes the work required to deliver and transition the result.  
B. They are always identical.  
C. Project scope includes only management work.  
D. Product scope includes the schedule.

**Correct response:** A.

### Question 3

What is the purpose of acceptance criteria?

A. Increase documentation.  
B. Define observable conditions that demonstrate whether the item or deliverable is acceptable.  
C. Eliminate testing.  
D. Replace governance.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

A team adds an unapproved feature to delight the customer. What is the primary concern?

A. The feature may constitute gold plating and create unassessed scope, quality, cost, risk, and acceptance effects.  
B. Customers should never receive extra value.  
C. The project manager should hide it.  
D. The schedule is irrelevant.

**Correct response:** A.

## Closing summary, 36:20 to 37:30

**NARRATOR**

Scope management connects outcomes to product characteristics, project work, requirements, acceptance, and change.

Define boundaries and exclusions. Decompose work without losing the whole. Maintain a governed backlog. Establish testable acceptance and traceability. Analyze every proposed change across the integrated project system.

On the PMP exam, do not accept or reject a request reflexively. Clarify, classify, analyze, identify authority, decide through governance, update the plan, and verify the result.

**ON SCREEN**

> Define the outcome. Control the boundary. Trace the evidence. Govern the change.

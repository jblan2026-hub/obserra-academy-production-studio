**VISUAL**

4. Safety personnel discover that one emergency process was not tested under full load.
5. A local works council raises concerns about role changes.
6. Customer service receives complaints caused by new fulfillment rules.
7. A supplier learns that packaging requirements changed without notice.

**NARRATOR**

The project had stakeholders. The project did not identify all of them early enough to influence the decision.

Stakeholder management is not a communication list. It is a disciplined process for understanding who can affect the project, who is affected by it, what evidence they hold, what authority they possess, what value or harm they may experience, and how their involvement should change over time.

## Scene 3. Who is a stakeholder, 03:15 to 06:25

**VISUAL**

A project boundary expands through six stakeholder categories.

### Decision stakeholders

People or bodies authorized to approve, fund, prioritize, accept risk, change scope, or govern the project.

### Delivery stakeholders

Teams, suppliers, contractors, specialists, and partners who perform or enable the work.

### User and operational stakeholders

People who use, operate, support, maintain, administer, secure, or retire the result.

### Beneficiary and customer stakeholders

People or organizations expected to receive value or improved outcomes.

### Affected stakeholders

People whose work, safety, privacy, access, environment, rights, or opportunities may change, even when they do not use the product.

### Oversight stakeholders

Regulators, auditors, legal teams, control owners, boards, inspectors, certifying bodies, and public authorities.

**NARRATOR**

A stakeholder does not need formal authority to hold critical information or experience material impact.

A person can be highly affected and have little organizational power. A person can have strong authority but limited day to day involvement. A person can oppose the project for reasons that are rational within their responsibilities.

**ON SCREEN PRINCIPLE**

> Identify people by their relationship to the project's decisions, work, outcomes, risks, and consequences, not only by title.

## Scene 4. Stakeholder discovery methods, 06:25 to 09:45

**VISUAL**

An iterative discovery process appears.

1. Review the charter, business case, contracts, policies, regulatory obligations, organization charts, and operating model.
2. Map processes, products, data, facilities, systems, suppliers, users, and dependencies.
3. Interview the sponsor and known stakeholders.
4. Ask each stakeholder who else is affected or holds relevant evidence.
5. Observe the actual work, not only the documented process.
6. Review incidents, complaints, support requests, audit findings, and prior lessons.
7. Analyze geography, language, accessibility, workforce, cultural, and community implications.
8. Revisit the model after major decisions and changes.

**NARRATOR**

Stakeholder discovery should be recursive. Each engagement may reveal another stakeholder, dependency, or consequence.

**ORIGINAL EXAMPLE**

A data classification project begins with security, legal, privacy, and technology. Process observation reveals records teams, scientists, sales personnel, finance users, executive assistants, contractors, and merger integration teams who create or handle information differently.

Without these perspectives, the policy may be technically correct and operationally unusable.

## Scene 5. The stakeholder evidence model, 09:45 to 13:40

**VISUAL**

A stakeholder record appears with ten fields.

1. Stakeholder or group.
2. Relationship to project.
3. Authority and decision rights.
4. Degree and type of impact.
5. Interest and expected value.
6. Knowledge and evidence held.
7. Current attitude and concerns.
8. Influence channels and dependencies.
9. Engagement need, method, frequency, owner, and privacy classification.
10. Success evidence and review date.

**NARRATOR**

Stakeholder analysis should support decisions, not become an unsupported ranking of people.

Common dimensions include power, interest, influence, impact, support, knowledge, and urgency. These are analytical lenses, not permanent labels.

A stakeholder's position can change when:

1. New evidence appears.
2. The project moves to a new phase.
3. The stakeholder experiences an unexpected consequence.
4. Leadership or organizational structure changes.
5. A regulatory or contractual obligation changes.
6. A project decision alters value or risk.

**PRIVACY WARNING**

Stakeholder records can contain sensitive opinions, influence assessments, conflict history, or personal information. Access and retention must follow approved policy and legitimate purpose.

## Scene 6. Engagement is more than communication, 13:40 to 16:35

**VISUAL**

Five levels of involvement appear.

### Inform

Provide accurate, timely, and understandable information.

### Consult

Gather input before a decision or design choice.

### Involve

Include stakeholders in analysis, design, testing, or implementation.

### Collaborate

Share problem solving and development within defined authority.

### Empower or decide

Grant or recognize legitimate decision authority.

**NARRATOR**

The appropriate level depends on authority, impact, expertise, risk, and project phase.

A project manager should not promise decision authority to a group that does not have it. Equally, a project should not claim meaningful consultation when stakeholder input arrives after the decision is fixed.

**ON SCREEN DISTINCTION**

> Communication tells. Consultation asks. Involvement works together. Authority decides.

## Scene 7. Documented public case: VA electronic health record configuration, 16:35 to 21:15

**VISUAL**

A clearly labeled government program case appears using original Obserra graphics.

**SOURCE CARD**

U.S. Government Accountability Office  
Electronic Health Records: Ongoing Stakeholder Involvement Needed in the Department of Veterans Affairs' Modernization Effort  
GAO 20 473  
Published 5 June 2020  
Source ID: GAO VA EHR STAKEHOLDERS 2020

**NARRATOR**

GAO reported that the Department of Veterans Affairs established 18 electronic health record councils made up of clinicians, staff, and other experts. VA held eight national workshops between November 2018 and October 2019 to support configuration decisions. It also held local workshops at the two planned initial implementation sites.

The scale of participation demonstrates that stakeholder engagement can require a structured operating model, not one general meeting.

GAO also found that decision procedures did not always ensure involvement of all relevant facility stakeholders. GAO recommended that VA clarify terminology and provide adequate detail in local workshop descriptions to help identify and involve the appropriate stakeholders.

**NARRATOR**

This finding teaches a subtle lesson. Creating councils and workshops does not guarantee that every necessary stakeholder enters the correct decision at the correct time. Engagement design matters.

**APPLICATION QUESTIONS**

1. Are workshop titles and objectives clear enough to identify the right expertise?
2. Are local workflows represented, or only national policy perspectives?
3. Can participants trace how their input affected the decision?
4. Are decision rights visible?
5. Are absent stakeholders identified and resolved?

**LIMITATIONS CARD**

1. Federal healthcare modernization context.
2. Workshop count does not prove engagement quality.
3. The report does not prescribe one engagement model for all projects.
4. The recommendation remained subject to implementation evidence.
5. Learners must review the official report before professional use.

## Scene 8. Hidden stakeholders and unequal power, 21:15 to 23:50

**VISUAL**

A power and impact map appears. One quadrant shows low formal power and high impact.

**NARRATOR**

Some of the most affected stakeholders may have the least influence over the project.

Examples include:

1. Frontline users.
2. Temporary workers.
3. People with disabilities.
4. Small suppliers.
5. Communities near a project site.
6. Customers with limited alternatives.
7. Junior specialists.
8. Contractors who see operational risk but lack governance access.

Ethical engagement requires more than giving powerful stakeholders greater attention. It requires understanding who bears consequences and ensuring that material evidence reaches decision makers.

**PMI ETHICS CONNECTION**

Respect requires listening to affected people. Fairness requires transparent criteria and appropriate opportunity to participate. Honesty requires accurate representation of what influence the engagement actually provides.

## Scene 9. Original enterprise scenario: Aurora Distribution Network, 23:50 to 28:20

**LABEL**

Obserra original synthetic scenario. Not a documented public case.

**SCENARIO**

Aurora is automating ten distribution centers.

Known stakeholders include the sponsor, automation vendor, technology team, safety director, and site managers.

During design review, the project manager learns that:

1. Night shift supervisors were not included.
2. Maintenance technicians have different diagnostic access needs.
3. Temporary workers represent 40 percent of seasonal staffing.
4. Two sites operate under different workforce consultation requirements.
5. Customer service is not represented even though fulfillment rules will change.
6. A packaging supplier must change labels and dimensions.

**QUESTION**

What should the project manager do next?

**OPTIONS**

A. Continue because the sponsor and site managers represent the organization.  
B. Add every person to every meeting.  
C. Update the stakeholder analysis, identify required representatives and decision points, tailor engagement by impact and authority, and assess resulting changes to requirements, risk, schedule, and transition.  
D. Ask the vendor to manage all additional stakeholders.

**NARRATOR**

Option C is strongest. The new evidence changes the stakeholder model and may change project decisions.

A assumes representation without verification. B creates participation without purpose. D transfers accountability to a supplier that may not hold organizational authority.

**FOLLOW UP**

The project creates stakeholder cohorts:

1. Operational design participants.
2. Safety and compliance reviewers.
3. Workforce and change participants.
4. Customer and supplier impact participants.
5. Authorized decision owners.

Each cohort has defined decisions, information, timing, owner, and feedback closure.

## Scene 10. Measuring engagement effectiveness, 28:20 to 30:20

**VISUAL**

An engagement dashboard changes from activity measures to effectiveness evidence.

### Activity measures

1. Meetings held.
2. Invitations sent.
3. Responses collected.
4. Training completed.

### Effectiveness measures

1. Critical stakeholder groups represented at the correct decision.
2. Input traced to decisions or documented rationale.
3. Unresolved concerns assigned and closed.
4. Stakeholder understanding verified.
5. Adoption and satisfaction trends.
6. Decision delay caused by late stakeholder discovery.
7. Requirements changes after implementation.
8. Complaints, exceptions, incidents, and workarounds.

**NARRATOR**

A full calendar does not prove engagement. The measure should show whether the project received relevant evidence, improved decision quality, protected affected people, and built operational readiness.

## Scene 11. Common PMP stakeholder traps, 30:20 to 32:10

### Trap 1. Engage only powerful supporters

Identify affected and knowledgeable stakeholders, including those with low formal power.

### Trap 2. Wait until implementation to involve users

Engage stakeholders when their evidence can influence design and planning.

### Trap 3. Treat resistance as ignorance

Understand the stakeholder's interests, impacts, evidence, and constraints before selecting a response.

### Trap 4. Communicate more without changing participation

A communication problem and an involvement problem require different actions.

### Trap 5. Use one engagement method for everyone

Tailor content, channel, frequency, accessibility, language, timing, and level of participation.

### Trap 6. Freeze the stakeholder register

Update it when project phases, decisions, risks, organizations, or external conditions change.

## Knowledge check package

### Question 1

A project manager discovers that frontline technicians were not included in a system design that will change their work. What should the project manager do first?

A. Proceed because managers approved the design.  
B. Update the stakeholder analysis and engage appropriate representatives before finalizing the affected decisions.  
C. Send the technicians the completed design.  
D. Ask the vendor to decide whether their input is needed.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 2

Which statement best distinguishes consultation from decision authority?

A. Consultation guarantees that every recommendation is accepted.  
B. Consultation gathers input; decision authority determines who is authorized to decide.  
C. They are the same.  
D. Decision authority belongs to the largest stakeholder group.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 3

A stakeholder has little organizational power but will experience significant impact. What should the project manager do?

A. Exclude the stakeholder because influence is low.  
B. Tailor an appropriate engagement path so material impacts and evidence reach decision makers.  
C. Give the stakeholder executive approval authority.  
D. Record the stakeholder only after implementation.

**Correct response:** B.

### Question 4

Which measure most directly evaluates engagement effectiveness?

A. Number of meetings.  
B. Number of slides presented.  
C. Percentage of critical stakeholder groups involved before relevant decisions, with input traced to outcomes.  
D. Number of project emails.

**Correct response:** C.

## Closing summary, 32:10 to 33:20

**NARRATOR**

Stakeholder engagement begins with disciplined discovery. Identify people through their relationship to decisions, work, value, risk, and consequence. Analyze authority, impact, knowledge, interest, and attitude. Tailor how and when they participate. Verify that engagement changes the quality and readiness of the project.

On the PMP exam, when a stakeholder appears late, do not dismiss, overreact, or add everyone to every meeting. Update the analysis, understand impact and authority, engage appropriately, assess project effects, and use governance.

**ON SCREEN**

> Find the evidence. Include the affected. Clarify the authority. Close the loop.

# Sequence 1: Cold Open, One Program and Four Definitions of Success

## Timecode 00:00 to 03:10

### Picture

Black screen. A low orchestral note rises beneath a distant mechanical pulse. The official Obserra mark appears in gold, then dissolves into a cinematic aerial shot of a global company at night.

The camera moves through four operating environments that belong to the same enterprise transformation.

1. Finance leadership views a screen labeled `Reduce operating cost by 18 percent`.
2. Operations leadership views `Eliminate manual rework and shorten order cycle time`.
3. Technology leadership views `Complete platform migration by the fourth quarter`.
4. Customer leadership views `Increase first contact resolution and satisfaction`.

Each group uses the same project name, `Project Northstar`, but the dashboards point in different directions.

Cut to a program review. The sponsor says the project is on track because the technical migration is 72 percent complete. The operations leader says the project is failing because business processes have not changed. The product team is building features requested by regional executives. The project manager opens the charter, but the vision statement reads only:

> Replace the legacy platform.

The room becomes silent. The four definitions of success float above the command table as competing trajectories.

### Narrator

“One project can have one name and still contain four incompatible futures. When people do not share the same vision, teams optimize locally, stakeholders create conflicting expectations, scope expands without a coherent purpose, and leaders may declare success using measures that do not prove value.”

### On screen decision question

**What should the project manager do first?**

### Learner interaction

The learner selects one response.

1. Create a more detailed schedule so each workstream sees the same dates.
2. Ask the sponsor to choose one department and reject the other objectives.
3. Facilitate a shared vision and outcome alignment session with the sponsor and key stakeholders.
4. Add every stated objective to scope immediately.

The interface records the choice but does not reveal the answer until after the evidence sequence.

# Sequence 2: What the Examination Is Testing

## Timecode 03:10 to 05:30

### Picture

The project command table transforms into a three dimensional examination map. The People domain appears in gold. Task 1 expands into four visible responsibilities.

1. Create a shared vision among key stakeholders.
2. Promote the shared vision among key stakeholders.
3. Maintain the shared vision through regular communication and updates.
4. Investigate misunderstandings by determining their root causes.

The wording is presented as an original Obserra visual summary. Do not reproduce a protected PMI graphic.

### Narrator

“The July 2026 examination does not treat vision as a sentence written once and stored in a charter. The project leader creates it with stakeholders, promotes it, maintains it as conditions change, and investigates why people understand it differently.”

“A strong exam answer usually preserves collaboration and evidence. It does not assume that a sponsor statement automatically created shared understanding. It does not solve a vision problem by adding detail to the schedule. It does not accept every expectation as approved scope.”

### On screen exam lens

1. What future state is the project intended to create?
2. What measurable outcomes define value?
3. Which stakeholders must share the understanding?
4. What has changed since the vision was created?
5. What evidence shows misunderstanding?
6. Who has authority to approve a material change?

### Narrator

“In the examination, vision questions often hide inside stakeholder, scope, value, change, communication, and organizational adoption scenarios. Identify the underlying misalignment before choosing the action.”

# Sequence 3: Anatomy of a Decision Useful Vision

## Timecode 05:30 to 09:10

### Picture

A holographic vision canvas builds one layer at a time. Each layer connects to a business decision.

### Layer 1: Purpose

Why does the project exist?

Example: Reduce unauthorized access and shorten the time required to provide appropriate access to the workforce.

### Layer 2: Future state

What will be observably different?

Example: Access is role based, approved, traceable, timely, and removed when no longer needed.

### Layer 3: Stakeholder value

Who benefits, and what do they value?

1. Workforce: faster access without unnecessary friction.
2. Security: reduced excessive privilege and stronger verification.
3. Audit: complete evidence and accountable approvals.
4. Business leaders: faster onboarding and lower operating risk.
5. Customers: reduced likelihood of unauthorized data exposure.

### Layer 4: Outcomes and measures

How will the organization know that value was created?

1. Median access delivery time.
2. Percentage of access tied to approved roles.
3. Number and age of excessive privilege findings.
4. Percentage of termination access actions independently verified.
5. User experience and service performance.

### Layer 5: Boundaries and principles

What must remain true while value is delivered?

1. Legal and regulatory obligations are confirmed by qualified owners.
2. Least privilege and segregation of duties are preserved.
3. High impact access changes require appropriate authorization.
4. Evidence and rollback are maintained.
5. The delivery approach may adapt without abandoning governance.

### Layer 6: Review triggers

What changes require the vision to be reconsidered?

1. Strategy change.
2. New law or regulatory obligation.
3. Major technology constraint.
4. Material customer evidence.
5. Acquisition or divestiture.
6. Benefits deterioration.
7. Safety, security, quality, or ethical concern.

### Narrator

“A useful vision connects purpose, future state, stakeholder value, outcomes, boundaries, and review triggers. It is concise enough to guide decisions and specific enough to expose contradictions.”

“A vision is not a detailed scope statement. It does not list every deliverable. It gives stakeholders a shared basis for deciding which deliverables support the intended outcome.”

### On screen distinction

**Vision:** the intended future and value.  
**Objectives:** measurable results that advance the vision.  
**Scope or backlog:** the authorized work and product boundaries.  
**Plan:** how the work will be organized and delivered.  
**Benefits measures:** evidence that the intended value occurred.

# Sequence 5: Documented Public Case, Healthcare.gov as an Alignment and Oversight Lesson

## Timecode 12:20 to 16:40

### Picture

The screen changes to a documentary style evidence room. No synthetic imagery is presented as actual footage. The sequence uses licensed or public domain architectural establishing shots, original motion graphics, and source excerpts limited to short attributed phrases.

A timeline appears.

1. Planned system development.
2. Initial launch.
3. Widespread performance issues for consumers attempting to create accounts and enroll.
4. Corrective action, including increased capacity and additional software quality reviews.
5. GAO review and recommendations.

### Narrator

“GAO identified several problems that contributed to the initial development and deployment difficulties.”

### Evidence panels

1. Inadequate capacity planning.
2. Software coding errors that were not adequately corrected before launch.
3. Planned functionality that was not fully implemented before the initial launch.
4. Requirements that were not always approved or linked through the requirements structure.
5. Systems that were not fully tested before launch.
6. Test documentation missing key pass criteria.
7. Oversight constrained by an unreliable schedule, missing estimates of remaining work, outdated documentation, and inconsistent progress reviews.

### Narrator

“This is not a story about a single failed meeting or one missing document. It is a systems lesson. Business intent, requirements, functionality, capacity, testing, schedule evidence, and oversight must remain aligned.”

“The relevance to a common vision is disciplined alignment. Stakeholders must understand what the system is expected to achieve, what functionality and quality are required, what evidence demonstrates readiness, and what authority governs the launch decision.”

### Synthetic decision simulation, clearly labeled

`OB SERRA ORIGINAL REENACTMENT BASED ON PUBLIC AUDIT THEMES`

A project team reports that the technical build is nearly complete. Operations says the service cannot support expected volume. Testing says integration evidence is incomplete. Leadership wants to preserve the announced launch date.

### Learner prompt

“What should the project manager do first?”

1. Reword the status report to reduce concern.
2. Convene the accountable stakeholders to reconcile intended outcomes, readiness criteria, evidence gaps, and decision authority.
3. Add more developers without analyzing the constraint.
4. Declare the launch successful because development is nearly complete.

### Narrator after submission

“The strongest response brings the conflicting definitions of readiness into one evidence based decision. It does not assume that schedule progress proves operational value or quality.”

# Sequence 6: The Vision Alignment Workshop

## Timecode 16:40 to 21:20

### Picture

The learner enters a premium Obserra virtual workshop environment. A large gold table contains six evidence zones.

1. Strategic intent.
2. Stakeholder outcomes.
3. Current facts and constraints.
4. Conflicting assumptions.
5. Measures and acceptance evidence.
6. Decisions and owners.

### Narrator

“An alignment workshop is not a motivational speech. It is a controlled decision process.”

### Workshop step 1: Establish the decision

What must the group agree to today?

Example: Agree on the future state, priority outcomes, nonnegotiable constraints, and authority for resolving tradeoffs.

### Workshop step 2: Surface stakeholder evidence

Each stakeholder states:

1. Desired outcome.
2. Evidence supporting the need.
3. Constraints.
4. Risk if the outcome is not achieved.
5. Assumptions.
6. Authority or dependency.

### Workshop step 3: Identify contradictions

The interface highlights incompatible statements.

1. Fixed launch date and unbounded scope.
2. Maximum security and zero user friction.
3. Global standardization and unrestricted local customization.
4. Immediate benefits and no process change.

### Workshop step 4: Define the shared vision

The group creates one statement that includes purpose, future state, and value.

### Workshop step 5: Connect vision to measures

Every major outcome receives an owner, baseline, measure, target, evidence source, review frequency, and limitation.

### Workshop step 6: Record unresolved decisions

Unresolved tradeoffs are not hidden. They are assigned to the appropriate authority with a due date and required evidence.

### Narrator

“Alignment does not mean every person receives every preference. It means stakeholders understand the common purpose, the tradeoffs, the decision rights, and the basis for future choices.”

### Practical artifact

The learner completes a `Vision to Value Map` containing:

1. Project purpose.
2. Future state.
3. Stakeholder value.
4. Outcome measures.
5. Assumptions.
6. Constraints.
7. Decision rights.
8. Review triggers.
9. Misalignment indicators.
10. Communication plan.

# Sequence 7: Maintaining the Vision in Predictive, Adaptive, and Hybrid Work

## Timecode 21:20 to 24:40

### Picture

The command table divides into three project environments.

### Predictive environment

A regulated facility project has stable approval gates and a baseline. The vision remains stable, but the team refreshes outcome assumptions when supply constraints and safety evidence change.

### Adaptive environment

A customer product team delivers increments and uses feedback to refine the solution. The vision guides prioritization while the backlog changes. The team does not treat every requested feature as part of the vision.

### Hybrid environment

An enterprise platform program has a predictive infrastructure migration, adaptive product teams, and a regulated data workstream. The shared vision connects the workstreams, while each uses a fit for purpose delivery system.

### Narrator

“Maintaining a vision does not mean changing it whenever a stakeholder has a new idea. It means testing whether new evidence changes the intended future, value, or boundaries.”

### Exam reasoning panel

1. In predictive work, use the approved governance and change path when the vision materially changes.
2. In adaptive work, use feedback and product authority to refine priorities while preserving the product or project purpose.
3. In hybrid work, maintain one coherent value system and make the boundaries between delegated adaptation and formal change explicit.

# Sequence 8: Diagnosing Misunderstanding by Root Cause

## Timecode 24:40 to 27:40

### Picture

Four teams repeat the same words, `customer value`, but their definitions appear beneath them.

1. Sales means revenue growth.
2. Operations means reduced handling time.
3. Security means reduced exposure.
4. Product means adoption and satisfaction.

The interface shows five possible root causes.

1. Ambiguous language.
2. Missing stakeholder participation.
3. Conflicting incentives.
4. Changed strategy or external environment.
5. Communication without feedback.

### Narrator

“When stakeholders misunderstand the vision, do not assume that sending the same presentation again will solve the problem. Determine the root cause.”

### Original scenario

A project manager repeatedly communicates that the program will create a `single source of truth`. Data teams interpret this as one physical database. Business leaders interpret it as consistent definitions and governed access. Regional teams believe it means no local data. The misunderstanding is structural, not merely a lack of communication frequency.

### Strong response

The project manager facilitates definition, architecture, governance, and outcome clarification with the affected stakeholders. The vision is updated to state what consistency, ownership, access, and regional variation actually mean.

### Weak responses

1. Send the same slide deck again.
2. Tell the technical team to decide privately.
3. Add all interpretations to scope.
4. Escalate before understanding the disagreement.

# Sequence 9: Examination Reasoning and Distractor Analysis

## Timecode 27:40 to 30:25

### Picture

The original cold open returns. The four definitions of success remain above the command table.

### Original practice question

A transformation program is meeting technical milestones, but stakeholders disagree about the intended business outcome and are prioritizing conflicting work. What should the project manager do first?

1. Add all stakeholder requests to the plan.
2. Facilitate a shared vision and outcome alignment session with key stakeholders.
3. Ask the sponsor to write a new status report.
4. Continue delivery because the technical milestones are on schedule.

### Correct reasoning

Option 2 is strongest. The project manager first resolves the underlying misalignment through collaborative vision and outcome work. Detailed planning, scope decisions, and reporting should follow from a shared basis.

### Distractor wall

**Option 1:** Creates uncontrolled scope before agreement.  
**Option 3:** Changes communication output without resolving the underlying conflict.  
**Option 4:** Confuses technical progress with shared value and stakeholder alignment.

### Multiple response practice

Which two actions help sustain a common vision?

1. Review outcome measures and assumptions when significant evidence changes.
2. Prevent stakeholders from questioning the original charter.
3. Use regular communication and feedback to test shared understanding.
4. Add every new request to maintain stakeholder satisfaction.
5. Replace the vision with the detailed schedule.

Correct responses: 1 and 3.

### Exam cue

When the question presents different definitions of success, conflicting priorities, or local optimization, look for the vision, value, and alignment problem before selecting a schedule, scope, or escalation response.

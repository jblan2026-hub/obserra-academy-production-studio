## 1. Production intent

The PMP® Exam Preparation and Business Application course must not look or sound like a narrated slide deck. Each lesson should feel like a premium documentary episode built around a consequential project decision. The learner should see real environments, hear authoritative facts, understand the governing standard or guidance, watch a decision unfold, and then practice the same reasoning in an original business scenario.

The target is cinematic quality in service of comprehension. Visual spectacle is not the objective by itself. Every shot, graphic, sound cue, and transition must help the learner answer one of the following questions:

1. What is happening?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What evidence supports the conclusion?
4. What authority, standard, or professional expectation governs the decision?
5. What should a project leader do next?
6. How would the same reasoning appear in a PMP-style scenario?

The course must remain unmistakably Obserra: black and deep navy environments, gold authority accents, restrained holographic blue, clean typography, exact facts, visible provenance, and a persistent emphasis on accountable judgment.

## 4. Episode structure

A normal lesson should use a 10-part narrative structure.

### 1. Cold open: the decision

Open inside a real decision, not with a definition. Examples:

- an executive asks whether a project can still meet a regulatory deadline;
- a team discovers a dependency that invalidates the baseline;
- a sponsor wants an unapproved change implemented immediately;
- a vendor claims a milestone is complete but acceptance evidence is missing;
- a project manager must choose between protecting the team and satisfying an unrealistic demand.

The cold open should last 45 to 90 seconds and end with a single question: **What should the project leader do next?**

### 2. Mission objective

State the lesson objective, the PMP domain and task alignment, and the workplace application. Keep this under 40 seconds.

### 3. Authoritative grounding

Present the controlling source in a restrained evidence frame:

- source owner;
- title;
- publication or effective date;
- source type;
- lesson relevance;
- limitations.

Where a short quotation is essential, keep it brief and attribute it exactly. Otherwise paraphrase.

### 4. Fact and data sequence

Present at least three verified facts or data points. Every number must have:

- source identifier;
- observation or publication date;
- unit;
- scope;
- limitation.

Do not present a portfolio statistic as if it predicts every project.

### 5. Concept model

Use a visual model to explain the concept. The model should be original Obserra content and should reduce complexity rather than merely decorate the screen.

### 6. Documented public case

Explain a documented case using primary evidence. Separate:

- observed facts;
- analysis;
- reasonable lesson;
- uncertainty;
- what the case does not prove.

### 7. Original business scenario

Move the learner into an Obserra-created scenario from a realistic environment such as:

- enterprise resource planning;
- medical technology;
- cybersecurity transformation;
- cloud migration;
- government acquisition;
- manufacturing modernization;
- business continuity;
- product launch;
- merger integration;
- executive protection technology;
- AI deployment.

### 8. Decision analysis

Show multiple plausible actions. Explain why one action is strongest, why others are premature or weak, and how the answer changes when authority, evidence, urgency, or delivery approach changes.

### 9. Exam reasoning pattern

Teach a repeatable approach:

1. Identify the domain and task.
2. Separate facts from assumptions.
3. Identify the project manager's current authority.
4. Determine whether the problem requires analysis, collaboration, escalation, governance, or execution.
5. Prefer the action that addresses root cause, protects value, and follows the appropriate process.
6. Avoid answers that bypass people, evidence, authorization, or change control without an emergency basis.

### 10. Application and handoff

End with:

- one business artifact;
- one workbook action;
- one original knowledge check;
- one AI tutor prompt;
- one limitations reminder;
- the next lesson transition.

## 5. Cinematography

### 5.1 Narrative footage

Use controlled, realistic environments:

- executive command room;
- project war room;
- design review;
- regulated manufacturing floor;
- data center or operations center;
- construction or infrastructure environment;
- healthcare technology implementation;
- government program review;
- remote distributed-team collaboration.

Camera language:

- 24–35 mm lenses for environmental context;
- 50–85 mm lenses for decision and emotion;
- slow, deliberate camera movement;
- motivated practical lighting;
- shallow depth of field for narrative scenes;
- crisp deep-focus graphics for analytical segments;
- no excessive handheld movement;
- no rapid cuts that undermine comprehension.

### 5.2 Reenactments

Staged or AI-generated reenactments must be labeled. They may illustrate:

- a project review;
- stakeholder conflict;
- procurement negotiation;
- change-control decision;
- schedule recovery meeting;
- risk escalation;
- lessons-learned workshop.

They must not falsely depict an actual person, company, regulator, or event.

### 5.3 Data graphics

Use 3D only when it clarifies relationships. Preferred graphics include:

- animated dependency networks;
- critical path emphasis;
- forecast cones and uncertainty bands;
- cost and schedule variance trends;
- stakeholder influence maps;
- domain-weighting radial chart;
- predictive/adaptive/hybrid spectrum;
- governance decision tree;
- risk-to-issue transition;
- value-delivery chain;
- benefits realization timeline.

Every fact card must be readable at 1080p and mobile scale.

## 6. Sound and music

### 6.1 Narration

Narration must be authoritative, calm, and human. It should sound like an experienced project executive teaching judgment, not an advertisement or synthetic reading.

Requirements:

- professional voice or owner-approved AI voice;
- accurate pronunciation of standards and project terminology;
- 135–155 spoken words per minute for complex explanation;
- controlled pauses before major decisions;
- separate pickup recording for corrections;
- no unsupported emotional exaggeration.

### 6.2 Dialogue

Scenario dialogue must be natural and concise. Characters should reveal competing incentives and incomplete information without becoming caricatures.

### 6.3 Music

Music supports tension and transition but never competes with instruction.

- dark cinematic pulse for cold opens;
- restrained orchestral/electronic bed for evidence sequences;
- silence or reduced score during critical reasoning;
- gold-toned resolution motif at lesson close;
- licensed or original music only.

### 6.4 Mix

- 48 kHz master;
- dialogue intelligibility prioritized;
- streaming loudness normalized to the approved platform target;
- separate dialogue, music, effects, and narration stems;
- caption timing verified against final mix.

## 7. Facts, data, and source cards

Each lesson must contain a source manifest. The on-screen source card format is:

**SOURCE**  
Owner — Title  
Publication or effective date  
Source ID  
What it supports  
Limitations

Example:

> U.S. Government Accountability Office — NASA: Assessments of Major Projects  
> Published 1 July 2025  
> Source ID: GAO-NASA-MAJOR-PROJECTS-2025  
> Supports: portfolio cost and schedule performance example  
> Limitation: public-sector space acquisition data does not predict the performance of every private-sector project.

Fact graphics must never omit scope. For example, `four of 18 projects` must not be simplified to `22% of projects fail` because the source refers to a particular NASA portfolio and assessment period.

## 8. Public-case production rules

Documented cases are not morality plays. A case should show systems, constraints, uncertainty, and governance.

Required case structure:

1. Case source and date.
2. Project objective.
3. Known conditions.
4. Reported performance or event.
5. Contributing factors supported by the source.
6. Management response.
7. Lesson relevant to the current objective.
8. Limitation and alternative interpretations.

The course should not imply that a single project-management technique would have guaranteed success. The learner should understand that complex outcomes often result from interacting technical, commercial, organizational, political, financial, and environmental factors.

## 11. AI tutor integration

At the end of every lesson, the Obserrian PMP Learning Coach should appear as a compact, branded learning dock. The learner may:

- request a simpler explanation;
- request a deeper explanation;
- ask for another original scenario;
- practice with a new ungraded question;
- apply the lesson to a business situation;
- add the lesson to a study plan;
- request a source-grounded summary.

During an active graded assessment, the tutor switches to integrity mode and cannot identify correct answers or evaluate options. After submission, it may explain the governing concept and provide targeted remediation.

The tutor must cite source identifiers from `authoritative-sources.json` and must state when an answer is based on Obserra original interpretation or a synthetic scenario.

## 14. Quality threshold

A lesson is not cinematic merely because it includes a dramatic soundtrack or generated imagery. It must meet all of the following:

- the learner can state the decision and why it matters;
- every important factual claim is attributable;
- the documented case is accurately represented;
- the scenario feels plausible to an experienced practitioner;
- the graphics improve understanding;
- the narration is precise and natural;
- the sound is professional;
- the brand is consistent;
- the learner can apply the concept at work;
- the learner can recognize the concept in an original exam-style scenario;
- the AI tutor can provide grounded follow-up without compromising assessment integrity.

That is the Obserra production standard for this course and the migration target for the rest of the Academy catalog.

## Cold open: compliant by checklist

A project reports 100 percent compliance because every checklist field is marked complete. Review reveals that one field cites an obsolete requirement, another has no evidence, a third applies to a different jurisdiction, and a critical exception expired two months earlier.

**NARRATOR**

Compliance is not the completion of a generic checklist. It is the governed process of determining what applies, translating obligations into project requirements and controls, producing evidence, managing exceptions, and monitoring change.

## Learning objectives

Learners will be able to:

1. Identify sources of project obligations.
2. Establish an applicability and traceability process.
3. Translate obligations into requirements, controls, tests, and evidence.
4. Integrate compliance into scope, schedule, cost, procurement, quality, and risk.
5. Manage exceptions and noncompliance under authority.
6. Monitor regulatory and policy change.
7. Communicate compliance status without unsupported claims.

**Sources:** PMI ECO 2026, ISO 21502, ISO 10006, NIST CSF 2.0, CISA supply-chain guidance, PMI Ethics.

## Sources of obligation

1. Statute and regulation.
2. Permit, license, or regulatory approval.
3. Contract and customer commitment.
4. Court or administrative order.
5. Organizational policy and standard.
6. Professional or technical standard adopted by the organization.
7. Security, privacy, safety, quality, accessibility, environmental, and records requirements.
8. Supplier and market-access conditions.
9. Voluntary framework selected through governance.

**NARRATOR**

A standard or framework does not automatically create a legal obligation. An organization may adopt it through policy, contract, regulation, customer commitment, or governance.

## Applicability record

1. Source and version.
2. Jurisdiction and scope.
3. Applicability decision and qualified owner.
4. Effective date.
5. Requirement and interpretation.
6. Affected deliverables, data, locations, suppliers, and stakeholders.
7. Control and implementation owner.
8. Test and evidence.
9. Exception and residual risk.
10. Monitoring and update date.

The project manager coordinates traceability but should not make legal interpretations reserved to qualified roles.

## Compliance by design

Integrate obligations into:

1. Requirements and acceptance criteria.
2. Architecture and design.
3. Work breakdown or backlog.
4. Schedule and review gates.
5. Cost and resource plans.
6. Supplier requirements and contracts.
7. Testing and evidence.
8. Training and operating procedures.
9. Release and transition.
10. Monitoring and records.

Late compliance review often creates rework because design and supplier decisions are already constrained.

## Evidence quality

Evidence should show:

1. Which requirement is addressed.
2. What control or action occurred.
3. Who performed and reviewed it.
4. When and on which version or scope.
5. Source integrity and retention.
6. Result and limitations.
7. Exceptions and corrective action.
8. Independent validation when required.

A policy document alone does not prove implementation. A test result from the wrong environment may not support release. An expired supplier report may not describe current risk.

## Exceptions

A governed exception should include:

1. Requirement and scope.
2. Reason.
3. Risk and stakeholder impact.
4. Compensating controls.
5. Authorized approver.
6. Effective and expiration dates.
7. Remediation owner and milestone.
8. Monitoring and revocation conditions.
9. Evidence and audit trail.

The project manager should not convert an expired exception into an assumed permanent approval.

## Noncompliance response

1. Preserve evidence.
2. Protect people, systems, data, and environment.
3. Notify authorized legal, compliance, safety, security, or regulatory roles.
4. Assess scope, severity, timing, and consequence.
5. Contain immediate exposure.
6. Determine reporting and notification obligations through qualified authority.
7. Correct the issue.
8. Address root cause and recurrence.
9. Update schedule, cost, risk, stakeholders, and forecast.
10. Verify closure.

## Cybersecurity framework example

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 is voluntary guidance unless adopted through an applicable obligation. It can help organize governance, risk, protection, detection, response, recovery, and supply-chain considerations. The project still needs an applicability decision and defined controls and evidence.

CISA supply-chain guidance can inform procurement and supplier risk, but it should not be presented as a universal contract or regulatory mandate.

## Original scenario: Northstar Regional Launch

Northstar plans to launch a customer-data platform in three regions.

1. Legal has confirmed different retention conditions.
2. The backlog uses one global deletion feature.
3. A supplier subprocessor was added after contracting.
4. The security test covers only one region.
5. A privacy exception expired.
6. The sponsor asks the project manager to launch and “document the gaps later.”

**Strongest action:** Preserve the evidence, engage authorized legal, privacy, security, supplier, and governance owners, determine the release impact, complete or govern required controls and exceptions, update the plan, and obtain the authorized release decision.

The organization delays one region, updates supplier terms and data flow, refreshes testing, extends one exception through proper authority, and documents residual risk and verification.

## Monitoring change

The project should monitor:

1. New or amended requirements.
2. Effective dates and transition periods.
3. Regulatory guidance.
4. Contract amendments.
5. Policy changes.
6. Supplier and component changes.
7. New jurisdictions or use cases.
8. Audit and incident findings.
9. Expiring exceptions and evidence.
10. Changes in project scope or technology.

## Common exam traps

1. Ask the project manager to interpret law alone.
2. Treat a framework as automatic legal compliance.
3. Defer compliance until final testing.
4. Launch with an expired exception because remediation is planned.
5. Claim compliance based on a checklist or policy only.
6. Ignore supplier or jurisdiction changes.
7. Hide a gap to protect schedule.

## Knowledge checks

1. A requirement may apply but interpretation is unclear. **Engage qualified legal or compliance authority and preserve traceability.**
2. An exception expired. **Stop relying on it and use the approved exception or remediation process.**
3. A framework is voluntary. **Use it as guidance unless adopted through an obligation.**
4. A compliance issue is found before release. **Assess, contain, govern, correct, update, and verify.**

Plan compliance from the beginning. Determine what applies through qualified authority. Translate obligations into requirements, controls, tests, evidence, and monitoring. Govern exceptions and communicate status truthfully.
